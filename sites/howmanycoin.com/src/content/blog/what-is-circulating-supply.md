Supply is one of the most fundamental drivers of value in any market. In cryptocurrency, the concept of supply is more nuanced than in most asset classes because it comes in multiple distinct forms — and confusing them leads to misreading data on any crypto information site.

Circulating supply is the figure that matters most for day-to-day market analysis. Here is what it means, why it is never fully static, and how it connects to the price of any given coin.

## What Circulating Supply Actually Means

Circulating supply is the number of coins or tokens that are currently accessible and active in the market — available to be bought, sold, or transferred between wallets.

It explicitly excludes:

- **Locked tokens** — coins held in smart contract vesting schedules, team allocations, or investor lockups that cannot be moved until a specified date or condition is met.
- **Burned tokens** — coins that have been permanently destroyed by sending them to an address with no known private key (sometimes called a "burn address"). These coins are mathematically provable to be permanently inaccessible.
- **Reserved/unmined supply** — for proof-of-work networks like Bitcoin, coins that have not yet been issued as mining rewards.

The formula for market cap uses circulating supply specifically because it represents the coins that are actively being priced by the market. Total supply or max supply would produce a number that includes value that isn't actually tradeable.

## Why Circulating Supply Changes Over Time

Unlike the supply of a physical commodity, cryptocurrency circulating supply changes on a continuous — sometimes daily — basis, for several reasons:

**Mining and staking rewards.** Proof-of-work coins (Bitcoin, Litecoin) add new coins to circulation each time a block is mined. Proof-of-stake networks (Ethereum post-Merge, Solana) add new coins through validator rewards. These additions are small relative to total supply but constant, which means circulating supply creeps upward on every network that issues new coins.

**Token unlocks.** Most newer cryptocurrency projects launch with a portion of tokens allocated to founders, early investors, and ecosystem development funds. These allocations unlock on a vesting schedule — perhaps monthly over two to four years. Each unlock event moves tokens from total supply into circulating supply, increasing the number of coins in the market. Data aggregators like [CoinGecko](https://www.coingecko.com) and [CoinMarketCap](https://coinmarketcap.com) track these schedules for major projects.

**Token burning.** Some protocols reduce circulating supply deliberately through burning. Ethereum implemented a fee-burning mechanism (EIP-1559) in 2021, which permanently destroys a portion of every transaction fee. Since the Merge, Ethereum has burned substantial amounts of ETH, partially offsetting new issuance. When burns exceed new issuance, a network becomes net deflationary — its circulating supply actually decreases over time.

**Staking lockups and liquidity withdrawals.** When users stake tokens in proof-of-stake systems, those tokens may be considered "locked" for reporting purposes. Conversely, unstaking moves them back into the liquid market. This creates a dynamic where circulating supply figures can fluctuate without any new coins being created or destroyed.

## The Three Supply Types: Circulating, Total, Max

To fully understand any crypto asset's supply dynamics, you need all three figures:

| Term | Definition | Example (Bitcoin) |
|------|-----------|-------------------|
| Circulating supply | Coins actively in the market | ~19.8 million BTC |
| Total supply | All coins created minus burned | ~19.8 million BTC (very close for BTC) |
| Max supply | Maximum coins that will ever exist | 21 million BTC (hard cap) |

For Bitcoin, these three numbers converge because: no coins have been burned (or very few), there are no locked team allocations, and the remaining supply to be mined is a known quantity built into the protocol. Bitcoin's transparency in this regard is part of what makes it easier to analyze than many newer tokens.

For a typical newer token, the picture is often quite different: circulating supply might be 30% of max supply, with 70% locked in various team and investor wallets. That gap represents future supply pressure.

## How Supply Affects Price: The Scarcity Logic

Supply alone does not determine price — demand matters at least as much. But supply dynamics shape the conditions under which price forms.

A coin with a hard cap (maximum supply limit) cannot be inflated by whoever controls the protocol. Bitcoin's 21 million hard cap is its most cited supply property. Defenders of this design argue that predictable scarcity makes the asset resistant to debasement over time — though this is a structural claim, not a performance guarantee.

A coin with no hard cap — like Ethereum — can theoretically issue coins indefinitely. In practice, Ethereum's post-Merge design aims for supply equilibrium or mild deflation through its burn mechanism, but the protocol's governance can alter issuance rules. Ethereum's lack of a hard cap is often cited as a philosophical distinction from Bitcoin, not necessarily a flaw.

**Inflationary tokenomics** describes assets that grow their supply over time, diluting existing holders if demand does not keep pace. Many proof-of-stake networks are mildly inflationary by design, as validator rewards require new issuance.

**Deflationary tokenomics** describes assets whose supply shrinks over time, either through burning, buybacks, or a fixed supply that is never increased. Deflationary designs create the structural conditions for scarcity but do not guarantee price appreciation — demand for the asset must also exist.

## Bitcoin vs. Ethereum: A Supply Comparison

Bitcoin's supply is among the most transparent of any cryptocurrency. The protocol enforces a hard cap of 21 million BTC. New coins enter circulation via mining, but the issuance rate is cut in half approximately every four years in an event called the halving. As of 2024 (the most recent halving), the reward per block dropped to 3.125 BTC. Eventually, around 2140, the last satoshi will be mined and issuance drops to zero.

Ethereum has no hard cap on total supply. After the Merge in 2022 — Ethereum's transition from proof-of-work to proof-of-stake — daily issuance dropped by roughly 90% compared to the mining era. Simultaneously, EIP-1559's burn mechanism destroys ETH with each transaction. During periods of high network activity, burns can outpace new issuance, causing net supply contraction. The Ethereum Foundation publishes data on issuance and burn rates; [ultrasound.money](https://ultrasound.money) tracks this in real time.

These design differences mean that analyzing circulating supply for Bitcoin and Ethereum requires different frameworks. Bitcoin's supply path is fully known in advance. Ethereum's supply path depends on network usage and governance decisions.

## Why This Matters When Using a Conversion Calculator

When you use a tool to calculate how many coins you receive for a given dollar amount, you are implicitly interacting with supply data. The price shown reflects what the market is currently paying for each unit of that asset — a price shaped in part by how many units exist. Understanding supply dynamics helps you contextualize whether the coin count you see represents a scarce asset, an abundantly issued token, or something in between.
