When people compare cryptocurrencies, they often fixate on price per coin. A coin priced at $50,000 sounds more valuable than one priced at $0.001. That intuition is usually wrong. Market capitalization — market cap for short — is the metric that actually tells you the relative size of a cryptocurrency network. Understanding it is one of the most useful things a newcomer to crypto can do.

## The Definition

Market cap in crypto is calculated with a single formula:

For more on this topic, see [*How to Calculate Crypto Market Cap (Step by Step)*](/blog/how-to-calculate-crypto-market-cap).

**Market Cap = Current Price × Circulating Supply**

Circulating supply is the number of coins or tokens that are currently out in the market — not locked, not reserved, not yet mined. Multiply that count by the current trading price and you get the market cap: a rough measure of the total market value of that asset.

For more on this topic, see [*Circulating Supply in Crypto: What It Is and Why It Changes*](/blog/what-is-circulating-supply).

Data aggregators like [CoinGecko](https://www.coingecko.com) and [CoinMarketCap](https://coinmarketcap.com) display this figure in real time for thousands of cryptocurrencies.

## Why a $5 Coin Can Be "Bigger" Than a $50,000 One

Consider two hypothetical coins:

- **Coin A** trades at $50,000 per coin, but only 10,000 coins exist in circulation. Market cap: $500 million.
- **Coin B** trades at $5 per coin, but 1 billion coins are in circulation. Market cap: $5 billion.

Coin B is ten times larger by market cap, even though its per-coin price is a tiny fraction of Coin A's. This is why price alone is a misleading comparison. Market cap gives you a more meaningful sense of scale — how much capital is collectively invested in the network.

Bitcoin illustrates this well. Its price per coin is high precisely because its circulating supply is capped and relatively small. That scarcity is built into the protocol.

## The Three Market Cap Tiers

The crypto industry generally sorts assets into three tiers based on market cap, though no universal threshold exists:

**Large-cap** — Typically above $10 billion. These are the most established networks with high liquidity and significant developer activity. Bitcoin and Ethereum are the canonical examples. They tend to see lower volatility (relative to the broader crypto market) because their markets are deeper and harder to move.

**Mid-cap** — Roughly $1 billion to $10 billion. These assets have gained meaningful adoption but carry more risk than large-caps. Liquidity can thin out during market stress, making large trades more difficult to execute without moving the price.

**Small-cap** — Below $1 billion, and in many cases far below. This tier contains thousands of projects ranging from genuine early-stage protocols to outright scams. Small-cap assets can move dramatically on low trading volume, making them both higher-risk and capable of rapid price swings in either direction.

For more on this topic, see [*How Many Cryptocurrencies Exist? (And How to Think About Them)*](/blog/how-many-cryptocurrencies-exist).

Categorizing a coin by market cap is not a substitute for due diligence. A large market cap does not guarantee legitimacy or long-term survival, and a small market cap does not mean a project is worthless. It is a starting point for comparison, not a verdict.

## Limitations of Market Cap as a Metric

Market cap is useful, but it comes with several important caveats that even experienced traders overlook.

**Circulating supply can be opaque.** The figure used in the formula is the reported circulating supply. If a project's foundation holds large amounts of tokens in undisclosed wallets, or if tokens are technically "in circulation" but practically illiquid, the market cap overstates how much capital would be needed to actually acquire a meaningful share of the network.

**Fully diluted market cap (FDV) tells a different story.** Many cryptocurrencies have a maximum supply that is larger than their current circulating supply. Tokens may be reserved for team members, investors, or future ecosystem incentives, and they unlock on a vesting schedule over months or years. FDV is calculated by multiplying the current price by the *maximum* possible supply:

**FDV = Current Price × Max Supply**

If a coin has a market cap of $200 million but an FDV of $2 billion, it means the market is pricing in a significant future dilution. When those locked tokens eventually enter circulation, downward price pressure is possible — all else being equal — because supply increases. Checking FDV alongside market cap gives a more complete picture.

**Illiquid tokens inflate the number.** If a founding team holds 60% of all tokens and those tokens are locked, the market cap calculation still includes their full notional value. The actual amount of capital it would take to "buy out" the liquid portion of the market is much smaller. Some analysts use liquidity-adjusted market cap models for this reason, though no standard formula exists across the industry.

**Market cap doesn't capture utility or fundamentals.** Two networks with identical market caps might have radically different levels of real-world usage, developer activity, transaction volume, or revenue. Market cap measures market sentiment and price relative to supply — nothing more.

## Practical Use: Comparing Coins Responsibly

When you use a tool like howmanycoin.com to look up how many coins of a given asset you can acquire for a certain dollar amount, you're interacting with price and supply together. Market cap sits right at that intersection. Knowing whether you're buying into a large-cap, mid-cap, or small-cap asset helps set expectations about liquidity, volatility, and the depth of the market you're entering.

For research purposes, cross-referencing market cap with trading volume, FDV, and token unlock schedules gives a much richer picture than any single number on its own. CoinGecko and CoinMarketCap both publish these figures alongside market cap for most listed assets.
