The number of cryptocurrencies in existence is one of the most frequently asked — and surprisingly hard to answer — questions about the crypto space. The difficulty isn't a lack of data. It's that the number changes daily, the definition of "cryptocurrency" is contested, and the vast majority of listed assets are functionally inactive.

Here is a grounded overview of the landscape.

## How Many Are Tracked Right Now

As of early 2026, [CoinGecko](https://www.coingecko.com) tracks over 15,000 cryptocurrencies across its database. [CoinMarketCap](https://coinmarketcap.com) lists a similarly large number. Both sites update their listings continuously as new assets are submitted and reviewed.

These figures should be understood as the number of assets that have met each platform's minimum listing criteria — which primarily means having some publicly accessible price data and trading activity on at least one exchange. It is not a measure of active, legitimate, or valuable projects.

The number of total ever-created crypto tokens — including those never listed on any exchange, those deployed and abandoned on test networks, and those created purely for scams — is likely orders of magnitude higher and is practically uncountable.

## Why So Many Exist: The Low Barrier to Creation

Creating a new cryptocurrency is technically straightforward and often cheap. This is the primary reason so many exist.

**Forking existing code.** Most blockchains are open-source. Anyone can copy the Bitcoin or Ethereum codebase, make minor modifications, and launch a new chain. Hundreds of Bitcoin forks exist (Bitcoin Cash, Bitcoin SV, and many more obscure variants), each created at different points in the chain's history by developers who disagreed with the direction of the original project — or who simply saw an opportunity.

**ERC-20 token deployment.** Ethereum introduced the ERC-20 token standard, which allows anyone to create a new token on the Ethereum blockchain in minutes using a simple smart contract template. No new blockchain is needed — the token runs on Ethereum's infrastructure. The cost to deploy an ERC-20 token is typically a few dollars to a few hundred dollars in Ethereum gas fees depending on network conditions. This low barrier has produced tens of thousands of tokens, the majority of which serve no ongoing purpose.

**Other token standards.** Similar standards exist on Binance Smart Chain (BEP-20), Solana (SPL tokens), Avalanche, and virtually every other smart contract platform. The proliferation of platforms has multiplied the total count of tokens further.

## The Top 10 Concentration

Despite thousands of listed assets, market value is highly concentrated. Bitcoin (BTC) and Ethereum (ETH) alone typically represent 50–65% of total crypto market capitalization. The top 10 assets by market cap often represent 75–80% of the entire industry's value.

For more on this topic, see [*How to Calculate Crypto Market Cap (Step by Step)*](/blog/how-to-calculate-crypto-market-cap).

This concentration reflects genuine adoption differences. Bitcoin and Ethereum have years of network effects, developer activity, institutional participation, and infrastructure built around them. The vast majority of altcoins — assets other than Bitcoin — have a fraction of this established ecosystem.

"Bitcoin dominance" (BTC's percentage of total crypto market cap) is tracked as its own metric on CoinGecko and CoinMarketCap. It fluctuates based on market conditions but has historically remained in the 40–60% range.

## Categories of Cryptocurrency

Not all cryptocurrencies serve the same purpose. Understanding the major categories helps in navigating the landscape:

**Layer 1 blockchains.** These are base-layer networks that process and validate transactions natively — Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana, Avalanche, Cardano, and others. They are the foundation layer of their respective ecosystems. Analyzing a Layer 1 involves understanding its consensus mechanism, developer activity, transaction throughput, and fee structure.

**Layer 2 networks.** Layer 2s are protocols built on top of Layer 1 blockchains to improve scalability or reduce costs. Ethereum's Layer 2 ecosystem includes Arbitrum, Optimism, and Base. Many Layer 2 projects have their own native tokens used for governance or fee payment. They are not independent blockchains — their security ultimately derives from the underlying Layer 1.

**Stablecoins.** Designed to maintain a fixed value relative to a fiat currency (almost always USD). USDC and USDT are the largest by market cap and trade volume. Stablecoins dominate crypto trading volume because they serve as the "dollar equivalent" in markets that don't touch traditional banking rails.

**DeFi tokens.** Tokens associated with decentralized finance protocols — lending platforms, decentralized exchanges, yield aggregators. Examples include UNI (Uniswap), AAVE, and MKR (MakerDAO). These tokens often carry governance rights over their respective protocols.

**Meme coins.** Assets that originated as jokes or cultural references. Dogecoin (DOGE) is the original example, created in 2013 as a parody. Shiba Inu (SHIB) followed. This category has grown substantially and includes thousands of assets with no defined utility. Some have achieved large market caps through community enthusiasm; many more have collapsed to near zero.

**NFT and gaming tokens.** Assets tied to non-fungible token platforms, blockchain-based games, or virtual world projects. These tokens often have very narrow utility within a specific application.

## Signal vs. Noise: Distinguishing Real Projects From Pump-and-Dump Schemes

With thousands of assets, a significant proportion are scams, abandoned projects, or low-effort forks with no community or development. Common red flags:

**Anonymous teams with no track record.** Legitimate projects in the current environment usually disclose at least some team information that is verifiable — LinkedIn profiles, prior work, GitHub contributions. A completely anonymous team is not automatically a scam, but it warrants extra scrutiny.

**No whitepaper or vague documentation.** Serious cryptocurrency projects publish a whitepaper explaining the technical design, tokenomics, and intended use case. The absence of one, or a whitepaper with only marketing language and no technical content, is a warning sign.

**Unrealistic promised returns.** Any project that promises a specific rate of return, guarantees profits, or uses language like "100x guaranteed" is exhibiting characteristics of a scam. No crypto project can promise returns — this is also an IRS compliance concern, as it may indicate unregistered securities activity.

**Token distribution that benefits insiders.** If the founding team or early investors control a very large share of total supply (50%+), they have the ability to sell their holdings and collapse the price at any time. Check token allocation data on CoinGecko or the project's documentation.

**Pump-and-dump patterns.** A rapid price spike followed by an immediate crash, often associated with coordinated promotion on social media, is a classic pump-and-dump structure. These schemes are illegal under US securities law when they involve securities, and they are common in the low-cap crypto market.

For newcomers, limiting research to assets listed in the top 100 by market cap on CoinGecko or CoinMarketCap — and reading both the project documentation and independent analysis before forming any opinion — reduces (but does not eliminate) exposure to outright fraud.

The diversity of cryptocurrencies is real, and the innovation in the space is genuine. So is the volume of low-quality and fraudulent projects. Treating each asset as a distinct entity requiring independent evaluation is the only reliable approach.
