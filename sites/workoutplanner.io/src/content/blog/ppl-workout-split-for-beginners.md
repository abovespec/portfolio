# PPL Workout Split for Beginners: 6-Day Push Pull Legs Plan

Push/pull/legs — commonly called PPL — is one of the most popular training splits for a reason. It groups muscles logically, minimizes overlap between sessions, and scales from beginner to advanced without needing a complete program overhaul. This guide explains exactly how PPL works and gives you a full 6-day beginner program to follow.

## What PPL Means

The split divides all your training into three categories:

**Push** — muscles that push weight away from your body: chest, shoulders, triceps.

**Pull** — muscles that pull weight toward your body: back, rear delts, biceps.

**Legs** — everything below the waist: quads, hamstrings, glutes, calves.

By separating these groups, each muscle only works during its designated day and has 48+ hours of rest before it is trained again.

## Is PPL Good for Beginners?

The classic PPL argument is that it is best for intermediates because the 6-day version hits each muscle twice a week. For true beginners with no training history, a 3-day full body plan is often recommended first.

For more on this topic, see [*3 Day Workout Split for Beginners: Full Body Strength Plan*](/blog/3-day-workout-split-for-beginners-full-body-strength-plan).

That said, many beginners thrive on PPL immediately — especially if you have time to train 6 days, enjoy a structured daily schedule, and learn movements quickly. If you have already done 4–8 weeks of full body training, PPL is a natural and effective step up.

If six days feels like too much, simply run PPL three days a week instead of six. Train Push Monday, Pull Wednesday, Legs Friday and repeat the following week.

## The 6-Day PPL Schedule

| Day | Session |
|-----|---------|
| Monday | Push |
| Tuesday | Pull |
| Wednesday | Legs |
| Thursday | Push |
| Friday | Pull |
| Saturday | Legs |
| Sunday | Rest |

This runs each muscle twice per week with at least one full day separating the same session.

## Push Day

**Exercises:**
- Barbell bench press: 4 sets x 6-8 reps
- Overhead dumbbell press: 3 sets x 10-12 reps
- Incline dumbbell press: 3 sets x 10-12 reps
- Cable lateral raise: 3 sets x 15 reps
- Tricep pushdown (cable): 3 sets x 12-15 reps
- Overhead tricep extension (dumbbell): 3 sets x 12 reps

The bench press is your primary strength movement — it goes first when you are fresh. Everything after it is accessory volume.

## Pull Day

**Exercises:**
- Barbell row or cable row: 4 sets x 6-8 reps
- Lat pulldown: 3 sets x 10-12 reps
- Single-arm dumbbell row: 3 sets x 10-12 reps per side
- Face pull (cable): 3 sets x 15-20 reps
- Dumbbell curl: 3 sets x 12 reps
- Hammer curl: 2 sets x 12 reps

Face pulls are often skipped by beginners but they are critical for shoulder health, especially when you are doing heavy pressing twice a week.

## Leg Day

**Exercises:**
- Barbell back squat: 4 sets x 6-8 reps
- Romanian deadlift: 3 sets x 10-12 reps
- Leg press: 3 sets x 12-15 reps
- Leg curl machine: 3 sets x 12 reps
- Bulgarian split squat: 3 sets x 10 reps per leg
- Calf raise: 4 sets x 15-20 reps

Leg day is the hardest day for most people. Do not skip it and do not put it last in the week when fatigue is highest — schedule at least one rest day near it.

## How to Progress

PPL responds best to linear progression on the main compound lifts. Add 5 lbs to your squat and deadlift every week. Add 2.5 lbs to your bench and row every week. When you can no longer add weight every week, switch to adding one rep per set before bumping the load.

Use our [workout planner](/) to log every session and track when to add weight. Without logs, you will lose track of where you left off and your progress will stall.

## Common PPL Mistakes

**Training with too much fatigue.** Six days a week is a lot. If you are sleeping poorly, constantly sore, or dreading sessions, reduce to 4 or 5 days. Intensity matters more than frequency.

**Skipping the big compounds.** The bench press, barbell row, squat, and deadlift drive most of your progress. Accessory work builds on that base — it does not replace it.

**Doing too many sets per muscle.** Beginners do not need 20+ weekly sets per muscle group. Start with 12–15 sets per muscle per week and increase gradually over months.

**Neglecting posterior chain on push days.** Face pulls and rear delt work are small investments that protect your shoulders and posture long term.

## Adjusting for 3 or 4 Days

If you cannot train 6 days consistently, run PPL on a rotating basis:

- **3-day PPL:** Push/Pull/Legs repeating — each muscle hit once per week.
- **4-day PPL:** Push, Pull, Rest, Legs, Push, Rest, Rest — or any arrangement that works your schedule while preserving rest days.

Consistency over a 3-day schedule beats sporadic attendance on a 6-day schedule every time.

## When to Move On

PPL scales well. You can run a version of this split for years by increasing volume, introducing periodization, or specializing in lagging body parts. Most people graduate from this specific beginner program when weekly weight jumps are no longer possible on the main lifts — typically 3–6 months in. At that point, adjust rep ranges, add sets, and consider an intermediate program with planned deload weeks.

The logic of the split stays the same. That is why PPL has lasted as long as it has.
