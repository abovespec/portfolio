Markup and margin are both ways of expressing profit as a percentage. They are often confused — and sometimes used interchangeably — but they measure different things. Using the wrong one in a pricing decision can erode your profits without you realising it.

## The core difference

- **Markup** expresses profit as a percentage of *cost*.
- **Margin** expresses profit as a percentage of *revenue* (selling price).

Because the denominator is different, the same profit produces two different percentages. A product that costs $60 and sells for $100 has a $40 profit. That $40 is 66.7% of the cost, but only 40% of the selling price.

## Markup formula

```
Markup % = (Profit / Cost) × 100
```

Where:
- **Profit** = Selling Price − Cost
- **Cost** = what you paid to acquire or produce the item

### Markup example

A retailer buys a pair of trainers for $65 and sells them for $104.

```
Profit = $104 − $65 = $39
Markup % = ($39 / $65) × 100 = 60%
```

The retailer applied a 60% markup.

## Margin formula

```
Margin % = (Profit / Selling Price) × 100
```

### Margin example

Using the same numbers — cost $65, selling price $104, profit $39:

```
Margin % = ($39 / $104) × 100 = 37.5%
```

A 60% markup corresponds to a 37.5% margin. They describe the same transaction from two different perspectives.

## Conversion formulas

You can convert between markup and margin directly without needing to know the actual dollar amounts:

**Markup to Margin:**
```
Margin % = Markup % / (100 + Markup %) × 100
```

**Margin to Markup:**
```
Markup % = Margin % / (100 − Margin %) × 100
```

### Example: converting 50% markup to margin

```
Margin = 50 / (100 + 50) × 100 = 50 / 150 × 100 = 33.3%
```

A 50% markup = a 33.3% margin.

### Example: converting 40% margin to markup

```
Markup = 40 / (100 − 40) × 100 = 40 / 60 × 100 = 66.7%
```

A 40% margin = a 66.7% markup.

## Why the confusion matters in practice

### Pricing errors

Suppose a business owner wants a 30% margin on a product that costs $70. If they mistakenly apply 30% as a markup instead:

- 30% markup on $70 = $70 × 1.30 = $91 selling price
- Actual margin: `(21 / 91) × 100 = 23.1%`

They aimed for 30% margin but are only achieving 23.1%. On high-volume products, this gap is significant.

To achieve exactly 30% margin, the correct selling price is:

```
Selling Price = Cost / (1 − Margin %)
Selling Price = $70 / (1 − 0.30) = $70 / 0.70 = $100
```

### Comparing suppliers and competitors

Retailers often quote margins while manufacturers quote markups. If you do not know which basis is being used, you cannot meaningfully compare figures.

## A comparison table

| | Markup | Margin |
|---|---|---|
| Measures profit relative to | Cost | Selling price |
| Common user | Manufacturers, wholesalers | Retailers, accountants |
| Formula | `(Profit / Cost) × 100` | `(Profit / Revenue) × 100` |
| Always less than | No upper limit | 100% |

Markup can exceed 100% (e.g., an item that costs $10 and sells for $25 has a 150% markup). Margin can never exceed 100% because profit cannot exceed the selling price.

## Gross margin vs. net margin

When discussing margin in a business context, it is worth distinguishing between the two main types:

- **Gross margin:** `(Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold) / Revenue × 100`. This only accounts for the direct cost of the product, not operating expenses.
- **Net margin:** `(Net Profit / Revenue) × 100`. This accounts for all costs — salaries, rent, taxes, interest — and shows what the business truly keeps.

Markup calculations typically correspond to gross margin thinking, not net margin.

## Worked examples side by side

**Scenario:** A coffee shop buys bags of beans for $8 and sells retail bags for $20.

| Measure | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Profit | $20 − $8 | $12 |
| Markup % | ($12 / $8) × 100 | **150%** |
| Margin % | ($12 / $20) × 100 | **60%** |

Both are correct — they just answer different questions. The markup says: "We charge 150% more than we paid." The margin says: "60 cents of every dollar in revenue is profit."

## Which one should you use?

**Use markup** when you are setting prices from a cost base — you know what something costs and want to apply a profit multiplier. This is common in manufacturing, wholesale, and trades.

**Use margin** when you are analysing profitability against revenue, comparing performance across products or periods, or communicating with accountants and investors. Most financial reporting uses margin.
