## Compound Interest Calculator — Watch Your Money Multiply

Einstein reportedly called compound interest the eighth wonder of the world. Whether or not he said it, the math is real: money that earns returns on its own returns grows exponentially, not linearly. Our free compound interest calculator lets you model exactly how fast any amount can grow — and why starting early matters more than investing large.

### What This Calculator Models

Enter your **starting principal**, **annual interest rate**, **compounding frequency**, **regular contribution amount**, and **time horizon**. The calculator returns:

- **Final balance** — what your money grows to by end of the period
- **Total contributions** — the actual dollars you put in
- **Total interest earned** — what compounding added on top
- **Year-by-year growth table** — so you can see the acceleration that happens in later years

### Compounding Frequency: Why It Matters

The more often interest compounds, the more you earn — because each compounding period adds interest to a slightly larger base. Common frequencies and their effect on a $10,000 investment at 7% over 10 years:

| Frequency | Ending Balance |
|-----------|---------------|
| Annually | $19,672 |
| Quarterly | $19,890 |
| Monthly | $20,097 |
| Daily | $20,137 |

The difference between annual and daily compounding here is about $465. In a retirement account with $100,000 over 30 years, that gap becomes substantial.

### The Compound Interest Formula

The standard formula for compound interest with regular contributions is:

**A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) + PMT × [((1 + r/n)^(nt) − 1) / (r/n)]**

Where:
- **A** = final balance
- **P** = principal (initial deposit)
- **r** = annual interest rate (decimal)
- **n** = number of compounding periods per year
- **t** = time in years
- **PMT** = regular contribution per period

The calculator handles all of this math automatically. But understanding the formula helps you see the levers you can pull: rate, time, and contribution amount all dramatically affect the outcome.

### The Most Powerful Variable: Time

The compounding growth curve is not linear — it accelerates. The first decade of a 30-year investment looks modest. The final decade looks explosive. This is why financial planners emphasize starting early over investing large:

- **$5,000/year starting at age 25** at 7% grows to approximately **$1.07 million** by age 65
- **$10,000/year starting at age 45** at 7% grows to approximately **$436,000** by age 65

Twice the annual investment, starting 20 years later, yields less than half the outcome. Time is the primary input — not the amount.

### How to Use These Results

1. **Model your current accounts** — enter what you already have and your expected rate of return to see your projected balance at retirement or a goal date.
2. **Test contribution increases** — see how much more the balance grows if you add $50, $100, or $200 per month.
3. **Compare accounts** — run the same scenario with different interest rates (e.g., a 0.5% savings account vs. a 4.5% high-yield account vs. a 7% long-term equity return assumption).
4. **Inflation-adjust** — for long horizons, subtract ~3% from your return rate to model real (inflation-adjusted) purchasing power.

### Compound Interest vs. Simple Interest

With **simple interest**, you earn interest only on your original principal. A $10,000 deposit at 5% simple interest earns $500/year regardless of how long you hold it. Over 20 years: $10,000 + $10,000 interest = $20,000.

With **compound interest** at the same rate, that $10,000 becomes approximately $26,533 over 20 years — $6,533 more. The difference grows with time and rate. Most modern savings accounts, CDs, bonds, and investments compound interest.
