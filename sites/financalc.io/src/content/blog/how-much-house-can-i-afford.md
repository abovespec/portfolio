Buying a home is likely the largest financial transaction of your life. Before you fall in love with a listing — or worse, before you start looking at listings — you need a clear picture of what you can realistically afford. "Afford" in this context has two meanings: what a lender will approve, and what won't strain your finances for the next 30 years. The two are not always the same.

## The Two Affordability Rules Every Buyer Should Know

Mortgage lenders use standardized ratios to evaluate whether a borrower can reliably make payments. Understanding these ratios before you apply lets you reverse-engineer a budget.

### Rule 1 — The Front-End Ratio (Housing Cost Ratio)

**Maximum housing cost = 28% of gross monthly income**

This ratio covers your total monthly housing payment, commonly called PITI: principal, interest, taxes, and insurance.

If your gross monthly income is $8,000, the front-end limit is:  
$8,000 × 0.28 = **$2,240/month** for all housing costs

### Rule 2 — The Back-End Ratio (Total Debt-to-Income Ratio)

**Maximum total monthly debt = 36%–43% of gross monthly income**

This covers your housing payment *plus* all other recurring debt obligations: auto loans, student loans, credit card minimums, personal loans.

At $8,000 gross monthly income:  
$8,000 × 0.43 = **$3,440/month** maximum total debt service

([CFPB — Debt-to-income ratios for mortgages](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-debt-to-income-ratio-why-is-the-43-debt-to-income-ratio-important-en-1791/))

The conventional limit is 36% for a "qualified mortgage" (QM) under standard guidelines; FHA allows up to 43% (and sometimes higher with compensating factors). Lenders may approve higher ratios for borrowers with strong credit, substantial reserves, or other positive factors — but a higher approved amount is not always a wise purchase.

## From Income to Maximum Home Price

Working backward from these ratios to a home purchase price requires knowing your:
1. Gross monthly income
2. Existing monthly debt obligations
3. Down payment amount
4. Property tax rate in your target area
5. Estimated homeowner's insurance
6. Current mortgage interest rate

A simplified example for a household earning $100,000/year ($8,333/month gross):

| Input | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Gross monthly income | $8,333 |
| 28% front-end limit | $2,333 |
| Existing monthly debt (car + student loan) | $600 |
| Available for housing (back-end 43% = $3,583 − $600) | $2,983 |
| Binding constraint | $2,333 (front-end) |
| Estimated property tax + insurance + PMI | ~$600/month |
| Available for principal + interest | $1,733 |
| At 7% rate, 30-year term, $1,733/month → loan amount | ~$261,000 |
| With 10% down ($29,000) | **Home price ~$290,000** |

The exact number shifts significantly with interest rate, down payment, and local taxes. Use our [mortgage calculator](/tools/mortgage-calculator/) to model your specific inputs precisely.

## The Down Payment: How Much Do You Need?

| Down Payment | Conventional | FHA | VA / USDA |
|-------------|-------------|-----|-----------|
| Minimum | 3%–5% | 3.5% | 0% |
| To avoid PMI | 20% | N/A (always requires MIP) | N/A |

**Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI)** is required on conventional loans when you put down less than 20%. It typically costs 0.2%–2% of the loan amount per year, adding $100–$300/month to the payment on a typical loan. PMI can be cancelled once you reach 20% equity.

A larger down payment:
- Reduces your loan amount and monthly payment
- Eliminates PMI
- Lowers your interest rate in many cases (lower LTV = less lender risk)
- Reduces total interest paid over the life of the loan

However, emptying your savings for a larger down payment is dangerous. Most financial advisors recommend keeping 3–6 months of expenses in liquid savings after closing, plus a repair fund.

## Hidden Costs Buyers Often Underestimate

The mortgage payment is not the total cost of homeownership. Buyers consistently underestimate:

| Cost | Typical Range |
|------|---------------|
| Property taxes | 0.5%–2.5% of home value/year |
| Homeowner's insurance | $800–$2,500/year |
| HOA fees | $0–$1,000+/month |
| Maintenance and repairs | 1%–2% of home value/year |
| Closing costs | 2%–5% of purchase price |
| Moving costs | $1,000–$5,000+ |
| Utility cost changes | Varies |

A $350,000 home in a high-property-tax state with a $400/month HOA and $3,500/year in insurance costs $1,067/month in non-mortgage costs before a single repair. Budget for these before setting your target price.

## What You're Approved for vs. What You Should Buy

Lenders will often approve you for more than you should spend. Approval is based on maximum debt-service capacity, not on your other financial goals: retirement savings, college funds, travel, emergency reserves, or career flexibility.

A useful framework: after your mortgage payment, taxes, insurance, and other debt, do you still have enough for:
- 15% of gross income to retirement savings
- 3–6 months of expenses in liquid emergency savings
- Your other financial goals

If the answer is no, your target price is too high — regardless of what a lender approves.

## First Steps Before You Start Looking

1. **Check your credit score.** Scores above 740 qualify for the best rates. Check for errors and dispute any inaccuracies.
2. **Calculate your DTI.** Total monthly debt ÷ gross monthly income. Aim below 36%.
3. **Determine your down payment amount.** Factor in closing costs and reserves.
4. **Get pre-approved, not just pre-qualified.** Pre-approval requires documentation and gives a firmer estimate.
5. **Consult a HUD-approved housing counselor.** Free guidance is available through HUD's website — especially valuable for first-time buyers. ([HUD — Buying a Home](https://www.hud.gov/buying))
