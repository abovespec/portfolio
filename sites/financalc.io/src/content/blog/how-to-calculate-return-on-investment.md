Return on investment is the most commonly cited metric in investing, business, and personal finance. It's also one of the most frequently misused. The formula is simple, but what it includes — and what it doesn't — determines whether it's telling you something useful or giving you a misleading picture. This guide covers the math, real-world examples, and the limitations every investor should understand.

For more on this topic, see [*What Is ROI in Investing? A Plain-English Guide to Return on Investment*](/blog/what-is-roi-in-investing).

## The Basic ROI Formula

**ROI = (Net Return ÷ Cost of Investment) × 100**

Or equivalently:

**ROI = ((Current Value − Cost) ÷ Cost) × 100**

Where:
- **Net Return** = Current Value − Cost of Investment
- **Cost** = the total amount originally invested or spent
- The result is expressed as a percentage

This formula always answers the same question: for every dollar I put in, how many cents (or dollars) did I get back as profit?

For more on this topic, see [*The Compound Interest Formula Explained: Variables, Examples, and How to Use It*](/blog/compound-interest-formula-explained).

## Worked Example 1: Stock Investment

You buy 50 shares of a stock at $40 per share. Total cost: $2,000. Two years later, the shares are trading at $54. You sell all 50 shares for $2,700.

**ROI = ((2,700 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000) × 100**  
ROI = (700 ÷ 2,000) × 100  
**ROI = 35%**

You earned a 35% return on your investment. Note that this is the total return over the entire 2-year holding period — not per year. We'll address the time dimension in a moment.

## Worked Example 2: Real Estate

You purchase a rental property for $180,000. After two years, you sell it for $215,000. During those two years, you also collected $18,000 in rental income (net of expenses). Your total return is the capital gain plus the rental income.

Net Return = ($215,000 − $180,000) + $18,000 = $53,000

**ROI = (53,000 ÷ 180,000) × 100**  
**ROI = 29.4%**

Over two years, the property generated a 29.4% return. Including income is important — ignoring rental income would show only 19.4% and understate the actual performance.

## Worked Example 3: Negative ROI

You invest $5,000 in a small business venture. The business struggles and you recover only $3,200 when you exit.

**ROI = ((3,200 − 5,000) ÷ 5,000) × 100**  
ROI = (−1,800 ÷ 5,000) × 100  
**ROI = −36%**

A negative ROI means you lost money relative to the original investment. This is still useful information — it tells you exactly how much you lost as a proportion of what you put in.

## Annualized ROI: Accounting for Time

The basic ROI formula ignores how long the investment was held. A 35% ROI looks very different if it took 2 years versus 15 years. To compare investments held for different periods, use **annualized ROI**:

**Annualized ROI = [(1 + ROI)^(1/n) − 1] × 100**

Where n = number of years held.

**Example:** From the stock example above, ROI = 35% over 2 years.

Annualized ROI = [(1 + 0.35)^(1/2) − 1] × 100  
= [(1.35)^0.5 − 1] × 100  
= [1.1619 − 1] × 100  
**= 16.19% per year**

This 16.19% annualized return can now be meaningfully compared to another investment that returned, say, 12% per year over a different time period. The basic ROI of 35% can't be directly compared across different holding periods without this adjustment.

## Comparing Two Investments Using ROI

| Investment | Cost | Final Value | Income | Holding Period | Total ROI | Annualized ROI |
|------------|------|------------|--------|----------------|-----------|----------------|
| Stocks | $10,000 | $16,500 | $0 | 5 years | 65% | 10.5% |
| Rental property | $10,000 | $13,000 | $4,500 | 5 years | 75% | 11.8% |
| Bond fund | $10,000 | $10,000 | $2,500 | 5 years | 25% | 4.6% |

Without the annualized column, you might rank these correctly by total ROI. But the annualized figure is what allows apples-to-apples comparison when holding periods differ, and it's the form most commonly used by investment professionals.

## What ROI Includes vs. Excludes

Understanding what goes into your ROI calculation is as important as the formula itself.

**Should be included:**
- Capital gains (increase in value)
- Dividends and interest received
- Rental income
- Any other cash flows received from the investment

**Often forgotten:**
- Transaction costs (brokerage commissions, closing costs, agent fees)
- Maintenance and carrying costs for real estate
- Taxes on gains or income
- Opportunity cost

**Example with transaction costs:** In the stock example above, suppose you paid $20 in brokerage commissions to buy and $20 to sell.

Adjusted cost = $2,000 + $20 = $2,020  
Adjusted proceeds = $2,700 − $20 = $2,680

ROI = ((2,680 − 2,020) ÷ 2,020) × 100 = (660 ÷ 2,020) × 100 = **32.7%**

Compared to the unadjusted 35%, not a huge difference here — but for large transactions with significant fees (like real estate), ignoring costs can meaningfully overstate returns.

## Limitations of ROI as a Metric

ROI is widely used because it is simple and intuitive. But it has real weaknesses that matter when making investment decisions.

**1. It ignores risk.**  
A 20% ROI achieved by putting everything in a single speculative startup is not equivalent to a 20% ROI achieved through a diversified index fund. ROI measures outcome, not the uncertainty or volatility involved in reaching it. Two identical ROI figures can represent very different risk profiles.

**2. It doesn't account for the time value of money.**  
Receiving $5,000 in profit today is not the same as receiving $5,000 in profit in 10 years. The basic ROI formula treats them identically. For long-term investment comparisons, metrics like Net Present Value (NPV) or Internal Rate of Return (IRR) are more appropriate because they discount future cash flows.

**3. It can be manipulated by the choice of what to include.**  
Should you include the value of your own time spent managing a rental property? What about unrealized appreciation versus actual sale proceeds? Depending on what you count as "cost" and "return," you can get very different ROI figures for the same investment.

**4. It's a backward-looking number.**  
ROI tells you what happened. It says nothing about whether that performance will continue or whether the conditions that produced it still exist.

**5. It ignores compounding frequency.**  
Two investments both showing 50% total ROI over 5 years could have vastly different growth paths. One might have compounded smoothly; the other might have declined for 4 years and then surged in year 5. Annualized ROI partially addresses this, but cash flow timing still matters.

## When ROI Works Well

Despite its limitations, ROI remains a useful first-pass metric for:

- Quickly ranking multiple investment options with similar time horizons
- Evaluating whether a past investment met your target return
- Comparing business decisions (e.g., marketing spend vs. revenue generated)
- Communicating investment performance to non-technical audiences

For straightforward comparisons — did Stock A outperform Stock B over the same period? — ROI works well. For complex portfolio analysis, retirement planning, or evaluating leveraged real estate investments, use more sophisticated tools.

## The Bottom Line

ROI = (Net Return ÷ Cost) × 100. It's the most accessible measure of investment performance, and for straightforward comparisons it does the job well. To use it accurately:

- Include all relevant costs and all income received
- Convert to annualized ROI when comparing investments held over different time periods
- Remember that ROI says nothing about risk, time value, or compounding structure

ROI is a starting point, not a complete analysis. The best investors use it as one input among several — not as the final word on whether an investment is worth making.
