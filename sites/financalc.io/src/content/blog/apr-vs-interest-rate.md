When you apply for a mortgage, car loan, or credit card, lenders are required to disclose two separate rates: the **interest rate** and the **APR**. They're always shown together. They're almost never equal. And yet most borrowers don't know what distinguishes them — which means most borrowers can't accurately compare loan costs.

For more on this topic, see [*How Does Compound Interest Work? A Plain-English Guide*](/blog/how-does-compound-interest-work).

Here's what each number means, why they differ, and how to use both when evaluating any loan.

## The Interest Rate: The Base Cost of Borrowing

The **interest rate** (also called the nominal rate or note rate) is the percentage the lender charges for the money you borrow. It determines the amount of interest that accrues on your principal balance each period.

For a $200,000 mortgage at a 6.5% interest rate with a 30-year term, the interest rate alone determines that your monthly principal-and-interest payment is **$1,264**.

The interest rate tells you how much interest accumulates — but it doesn't tell you the full cost of the loan.

## The APR: The Full Cost of Borrowing

The **Annual Percentage Rate (APR)** includes the interest rate *plus* most lender fees and costs, expressed as a single annual percentage. It reflects what the loan actually costs you each year when fees are factored in.

Fees typically included in APR calculations:
- Origination fees
- Discount points (prepaid interest to buy down the rate)
- Mortgage broker fees
- Private mortgage insurance (PMI) in some cases
- Certain closing costs

Under the federal Truth in Lending Act (TILA) and Regulation Z, lenders are legally required to disclose APR on consumer loans. This requirement exists precisely so borrowers can make apples-to-apples comparisons. ([Federal Reserve — Regulation Z](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/regzcg.htm))

## Why APR is Always Equal to or Higher Than the Interest Rate

Because APR includes fees in addition to interest, it is always higher than the nominal interest rate — unless there are genuinely no additional fees.

Example: Two lenders offer you a $200,000 mortgage.

| Lender | Interest Rate | Fees | APR |
|--------|---------------|------|-----|
| Lender A | 6.50% | $0 | 6.50% |
| Lender B | 6.25% | $4,000 | 6.52% |

Lender B's interest rate looks lower — but its APR is slightly higher once the $4,000 in origination fees is factored in. Over a 30-year loan, Lender A is actually cheaper if you keep the loan to maturity.

This comparison only holds if you keep the loan long enough. If you sell or refinance in 5 years, you'll benefit from Lender B's lower rate before you've amortized the fee premium.

## APR on Credit Cards: How It Works Differently

For **revolving credit** (credit cards), APR works differently than for installment loans (mortgages, auto loans, personal loans).

Credit card APR is expressed annually but applied monthly. A card with 24% APR charges **2% per month** on your carried balance. There are typically no origination fees, so the credit card APR is essentially equal to the interest rate.

Credit cards often have **multiple APRs** for different transaction types:
- **Purchase APR:** Applied to regular purchases if you carry a balance
- **Balance transfer APR:** Often a promotional 0% for a period, then reverts to a higher rate
- **Cash advance APR:** Almost always higher than the purchase APR, with no grace period
- **Penalty APR:** A punitive rate triggered by late payments, sometimes 29.99%+

([CFPB — Credit Card APR](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-credit-card-s-annual-percentage-rate-apr-en-44/))

## Fixed APR vs. Variable APR

**Fixed APR** stays the same for the life of the loan. Predictable monthly payment, no surprises.

**Variable APR** is tied to a benchmark rate — typically the prime rate or the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR). When benchmark rates rise, your variable APR rises. When they fall, your variable APR falls.

Fixed rates offer certainty; variable rates can be lower initially but carry risk if rates increase.

## How to Use APR When Comparing Loans

1. **Compare APRs, not interest rates,** when evaluating loans of the same type and term. APR is the standardized comparison metric.
2. **Adjust for term differences.** A 30-year mortgage APR is not directly comparable to a 15-year mortgage APR — the fee amortization works differently.
3. **Consider your time horizon.** A loan with high upfront fees may have a higher APR but lower rate — it's only the better deal if you hold it long enough to amortize the fee premium.
4. **Watch for excluded costs.** APR doesn't include all costs — property taxes, homeowner's insurance, and title insurance are not included in mortgage APR.
5. **For credit cards, prioritize the lowest APR** if you carry balances. If you pay in full every month, APR is irrelevant — you're never charged interest.

## The Effective Annual Rate (EAR)

One further distinction: the **Effective Annual Rate (EAR)** accounts for intra-year compounding. APR is a simple annualization; EAR reflects what the loan actually costs when compounding is factored in.

For a loan at 12% APR compounded monthly, the EAR = (1 + 0.12/12)^12 − 1 = **12.68%**. For most consumer loans, this distinction is less significant than APR vs. interest rate, but it matters for precise comparisons.
