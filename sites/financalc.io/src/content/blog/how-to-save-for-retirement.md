Retirement feels abstract when it's 30 years away. But the math of compounding is unforgiving: every year you delay saving costs you more than a year's worth of contributions — it costs you all the future returns those contributions would have generated. The best time to start is now. The second best time was yesterday.

This guide walks through the key questions in sequence: how much you'll need, where to save it, how much to contribute, and what to do at different life stages.

## Step 1: Estimate How Much You'll Need

The starting point is your target annual income in retirement. A commonly used rule of thumb: **you'll need 70%–80% of your pre-retirement income** per year, adjusted for the fact that you won't have commuting costs, work clothing expenses, payroll taxes, or retirement savings contributions.

For more on this topic, see [*The Compound Interest Formula Explained: Variables, Examples, and How to Use It*](/blog/compound-interest-formula-explained).

For a household with $80,000/year in pre-retirement income, that suggests $56,000–$64,000/year in retirement income.

Once you have a target income, apply the **25× rule** (derived from the 4% safe withdrawal rate): multiply your target annual income by 25 to estimate your required portfolio size.

For more on this topic, see [*The Rule of 72 Explained: How to Estimate Doubling Time in Seconds*](/blog/rule-of-72-explained).

| Target Annual Income | Estimated Portfolio Needed |
|---------------------|---------------------------|
| $40,000 | $1,000,000 |
| $60,000 | $1,500,000 |
| $80,000 | $2,000,000 |
| $100,000 | $2,500,000 |

**Subtract Social Security.** If you'll receive $20,000/year from Social Security, your portfolio only needs to cover the remaining $40,000–$60,000 per year. Estimate your Social Security benefit at the [SSA official website](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/).

## Step 2: Choose the Right Accounts

### 401(k) / 403(b) — Start Here If Your Employer Offers It

A 401(k) (private sector) or 403(b) (nonprofit/government) is your first stop because of **employer matching** — free money that is an immediate return on your contribution.

**2024 contribution limits:** $23,000/year ($30,500 if age 50 or older).

Most plans offer a match — often 50%–100% of contributions up to 3%–6% of salary. Always contribute enough to capture the full match. Not doing so is leaving a guaranteed 50%–100% return on the table.

**Traditional 401(k):** Contributions are pre-tax. You reduce taxable income now; withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.

**Roth 401(k):** Contributions are after-tax. No immediate tax break, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free.

### Traditional IRA

An Individual Retirement Account you open yourself. **2024 contribution limit:** $7,000/year ($8,000 if age 50+).

Contributions may be tax-deductible depending on your income and whether you have a workplace plan. ([IRS — IRA Deduction Limits](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/ira-deduction-limits))

Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. Required minimum distributions (RMDs) begin at age 73.

### Roth IRA

Same contribution limits as Traditional IRA. Contributions are after-tax, but qualified withdrawals are **completely tax-free** — including all growth.

Income limits apply to direct Roth IRA contributions (phase-out begins at $146,000 for single filers, $230,000 for married filing jointly in 2024). Above the limit, a "backdoor Roth" strategy may be available — consult a tax professional.

**The Roth vs. Traditional decision** hinges on your current vs. expected future tax rate. If you expect to be in a higher bracket in retirement, Roth is generally better. If you're in your peak earning years and expect a lower bracket in retirement, traditional is often better.

## Step 3: How Much Should You Save?

A widely cited benchmark: **save 15% of gross income for retirement** (including employer match). This target, if started in your mid-20s, typically generates sufficient savings over a 40-year career.

| Age Started | Monthly Save Rate (for $1M by 65 at 7%) | Approx. Monthly $ on $60K Income |
|-------------|----------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| 25 | ~5% of income | ~$250 |
| 35 | ~10% of income | ~$500 |
| 45 | ~25% of income | ~$1,250 |
| 55 | ~55%+ of income | ~$2,750+ |

The later you start, the more aggressive the savings rate must be to catch up. If you're starting late, a combination of higher contributions, longer working years, and reduced retirement spending targets may all be necessary.

## Step 4: What to Invest In

Inside retirement accounts, the most important choice is asset allocation — the mix of stocks and bonds.

**Target-date funds** are the simplest option: pick the fund closest to your expected retirement year, and it automatically adjusts from aggressive (more stocks) to conservative (more bonds) as you approach retirement. They are widely available in 401(k) plans and are the default for many participants.

**For DIY investors:** A standard starting point is 90/10 (90% stocks, 10% bonds) in your 20s and 30s, gradually shifting toward 60/40 by retirement. Broad, low-cost index funds (total stock market, international, bond index) are the dominant evidence-based recommendation for most long-term retail investors.

Keep fees low. A 1% annual fund fee versus a 0.05% fee can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars over a 40-year period due to compounding. ([DOL — Savings Fitness Guide](https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/ebsa/about-ebsa/our-activities/resource-center/publications/savings-fitness.pdf))

## Step 5: Automate and Increase Over Time

The Federal Reserve's household finance surveys consistently show that automatic enrollment and automatic contribution escalation are the most effective behavioral tools for increasing retirement savings rates. ([Federal Reserve — Economic Well-Being Survey](https://www.federalreserve.gov/consumerscommunities/shed.htm))

**Automate:** Set contributions to come out of your paycheck before you see the money. Out of sight, out of mind.

**Escalate:** Increase your contribution rate by 1% every year, or whenever you get a raise. Escalating by 1% per year feels painless but compounds dramatically over a career.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Not capturing the full employer match.** This is the highest-priority error to fix first.
- **Cashing out when you change jobs.** Rolling over to an IRA or new employer plan preserves tax-advantaged growth. Early withdrawals trigger income tax plus a 10% penalty.
- **Holding too much in company stock.** Concentration risk in a single employer is a major vulnerability — you already depend on the company for income.
- **Waiting for the "right time" to invest.** Time in the market beats timing the market, as decades of financial research consistently show.
- **Not accounting for healthcare costs.** Healthcare is consistently the largest unplanned expense in retirement for most American households. Consider dedicated health savings (HSA if eligible) alongside retirement savings.
