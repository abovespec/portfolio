You're at a dinner party and someone asks how long it takes to double money at 6% interest. You don't have a calculator or a spreadsheet. With the Rule of 72, you answer in about two seconds: 12 years. And you'd be almost exactly right.

The Rule of 72 is one of the most useful mental math shortcuts in personal finance. It's not precise enough for professional financial modeling, but for quick estimation, sanity-checking investment claims, and building intuition about compound growth, nothing beats it.

## What the Rule of 72 Is

Divide 72 by an annual interest rate (as a whole number, not a decimal) and you get an approximation of how many years it takes an investment to double in value.

**Years to double ≈ 72 ÷ annual interest rate**

That's it. No exponents, no logarithms. A single division problem.

## Examples Across Common Interest Rates

| Annual Rate | Years to Double (Rule of 72) | Exact Years |
|-------------|------------------------------|-------------|
| 2% | 36 years | 35.0 years |
| 3% | 24 years | 23.4 years |
| 4% | 18 years | 17.7 years |
| 6% | 12 years | 11.9 years |
| 8% | 9 years | 9.0 years |
| 10% | 7.2 years | 7.3 years |
| 12% | 6 years | 6.1 years |
| 18% | 4 years | 4.2 years |
| 24% | 3 years | 3.2 years |

The rule is remarkably accurate between roughly 2% and 15%. At higher rates the estimate drifts slightly, but it remains a reliable ballpark.

## How to Use It in Practice

### Checking an investment claim

Someone tells you their investment doubles every 5 years. What return does that imply? Flip the formula: **Rate ≈ 72 ÷ years**. So 72 ÷ 5 = 14.4%. That's a high return for any conventional investment. If someone promises a consistent 14%+ return with little risk, that's worth scrutinizing carefully.

### Understanding debt cost

The Rule of 72 applies to debt too. A credit card charging 24% interest will double the amount owed — if you make no payments — in 72 ÷ 24 = **3 years**. A $5,000 balance becomes $10,000 in three years purely from interest. This framing makes the cost of high-interest debt viscerally clear.

### Comparing savings rates

If your savings account pays 0.5%, your money doubles in 72 ÷ 0.5 = **144 years**. A high-yield account at 4.5% doubles in 72 ÷ 4.5 = **16 years**. The same dollar in each account reaches $2,000 starting from $1,000 in 16 years vs. 144 years. That's the real cost of keeping money in a low-yield account.

### Planning retirement milestones

At an assumed 7% long-run stock market return, the Rule of 72 says money doubles roughly every 10 years. A 25-year-old with $10,000 in a retirement account can expect:

- Age 35: ~$20,000
- Age 45: ~$40,000
- Age 55: ~$80,000
- Age 65: ~$160,000

No additional contributions. Just the original $10,000 compounding. This makes the value of early investing immediately intuitive.

## Why 72? The Math Behind the Rule

The exact formula for doubling time using compound interest is:

**t = ln(2) / ln(1 + r)**

At moderate interest rates, ln(2) ≈ 0.693 and ln(1 + r) ≈ r, so the exact expression simplifies to approximately 0.693/r. To use whole-number rates instead of decimals, multiply both sides by 100, giving you 69.3/rate. The number 72 is used instead of 69.3 because 72 has many integer factors (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, 18, 24, 36, 72) — which makes mental division much easier — and it happens to give slightly more accurate results for rates in the 6%–10% range where the approximation benefits from a small upward adjustment.

## Variations: Rule of 70 and Rule of 69.3

Some texts use **Rule of 70** instead of Rule of 72. The difference is minor:

- Rule of 70 is slightly more accurate for continuously compounded interest or very low rates
- Rule of 72 is more accurate for typical discrete compounding in the 6%–10% range and offers easier mental division

For everyday financial conversations, 72 is the standard. For academic or continuous-compounding contexts, 69.3 is technically more precise.

## Limitations of the Rule of 72

The Rule of 72 is a quick estimation tool, not a planning calculator. Be aware of what it doesn't account for:

**1. Regular contributions.** The rule assumes a single lump sum. If you add money regularly, your actual doubling time is shorter — but the rule doesn't model this. Use a [financial calculator](/) for contribution-based projections.

**2. Variable returns.** The rule assumes a constant interest rate. In reality, investment returns fluctuate year to year. The rule gives an approximation based on a steady-state assumption.

**3. Taxes and fees.** The rule models gross returns. If your account charges a 0.5% expense ratio, or if interest is taxable, your effective rate is lower and your actual doubling time is longer.

**4. Inflation.** The rule tells you when your nominal dollar balance doubles — not when your purchasing power doubles. With 3% inflation, "real" doubling takes longer.

**5. Works best within a range.** Below 2% or above 20%, the approximation becomes less reliable. Use the exact formula (t = ln(2) / ln(1 + r)) when precision matters.

## The Rule of 72 for Inflation and Purchasing Power

The Rule of 72 can also work in reverse: how quickly does inflation cut your purchasing power in half?

At 3% inflation: 72 ÷ 3 = **24 years** before your money buys half as much.  
At 6% inflation: 72 ÷ 6 = **12 years**.  
At 8% inflation: 72 ÷ 8 = **9 years**.

This is why financial advisors emphasize investing in assets that outpace inflation. Cash sitting in a 0.5% savings account during 4% inflation loses purchasing power at the rate of about 3.5% per year — meaning its real value halves in about 20 years even while the nominal balance grows.

## The Bottom Line

The Rule of 72 gives you a powerful shortcut for thinking about compound growth without a calculator. At 6% your money doubles in 12 years. At 8% it doubles in 9. At 24% debt, what you owe doubles in 3. These numbers are easy to remember and hard to dismiss.

The rule works best as a gut-check and a conversation tool. For serious planning — retirement projections, loan comparisons, investment analysis — use a [compound interest calculator](/) to model exact figures with your actual rate, time horizon, and contribution schedule. But for building quick financial intuition, the Rule of 72 is one of the best tools you have.
