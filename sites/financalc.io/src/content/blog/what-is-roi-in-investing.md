Every investment decision comes down to a simple question: is this worth it? Return on investment (ROI) is the number that answers it. It expresses how much you gained or lost as a percentage of what you put in — giving you a common language to compare a rental property to a stock trade to a business expense.

For more on this topic, see [*The Compound Interest Formula Explained: Variables, Examples, and How to Use It*](/blog/compound-interest-formula-explained).

## The ROI Formula

**ROI = (Net Gain / Cost of Investment) × 100**

Or equivalently:

**ROI = ((Final Value − Initial Investment) / Initial Investment) × 100**

**Example:** You buy $5,000 worth of stock. It grows to $6,750. You sell.

Net gain = $6,750 − $5,000 = $1,750  
ROI = ($1,750 / $5,000) × 100 = **35%**

You earned 35 cents for every dollar invested. Clear, comparable, and useful.

## The Limitation of Simple ROI: It Ignores Time

A 35% ROI sounds identical whether it happened in one year or ten years — but the investment merit is completely different. A 35% return in one year is outstanding. The same return over ten years is underwhelming (roughly 3% per year).

This is why analysts almost always pair simple ROI with **annualized ROI**, which puts returns on a time-adjusted basis.

## Annualized ROI (CAGR)

**Annualized ROI = [(Final Value / Initial Value)^(1/n) − 1] × 100**

Where **n** = number of years.

Using the same $5,000 → $6,750 example:
- Over **2 years**: (6,750/5,000)^(1/2) − 1 = **16.2% per year**
- Over **5 years**: (6,750/5,000)^(1/5) − 1 = **6.2% per year**
- Over **10 years**: (6,750/5,000)^(1/10) − 1 = **3.0% per year**

This is also called the **Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR)**. It is the standard metric used in investment reports, fund prospectuses, and financial analysis to compare returns across different time periods. ([Investopedia — CAGR](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/c/cagr.asp))

## What Is a "Good" ROI?

There's no universal answer — it depends on asset class, risk level, and time horizon. Common benchmarks:

| Asset / Benchmark | Historical Return |
|-------------------|------------------|
| U.S. equities (S&P 500, long run) | ~10% nominal / ~7% real (inflation-adjusted) |
| U.S. bonds (aggregate) | ~3%–5% nominal |
| Real estate (residential, national avg) | ~4%–8% annualized |
| High-yield savings (2025–2026) | ~4%–5% |
| Starting a business (median) | Highly variable; many fail |

The key question is not "is 8% good?" in the abstract — it is "is 8% good given the risk I took?" A 35% return on a penny stock is not as impressive as a 12% return on a diversified index fund if the penny stock could have gone to zero.

**Risk-adjusted return** (measured by the Sharpe ratio and similar metrics) is the more complete picture for investment professionals, but for everyday decisions, comparing ROI to your alternatives is sufficient.

## ROI for Different Investment Types

### Stocks and Funds
ROI on a stock position = ((Sale Price + Dividends Received) − Purchase Price) / Purchase Price × 100

Don't forget to include dividends and distributions — they are part of your total return.

### Real Estate
Real estate ROI must include all costs, not just purchase and sale price:

**Net Gain = Sale Price − (Purchase Price + Renovation Costs + Carrying Costs + Selling Costs)**

A property bought for $200,000, renovated for $30,000, held for 2 years with $20,000 in carrying costs, sold for $310,000:

Net gain = $310,000 − ($200,000 + $30,000 + $20,000) = $60,000  
Simple ROI = $60,000 / $250,000 = **24%** over 2 years → **11.4% annualized**

### Business Investments
For a marketing campaign, capital equipment, or business expansion:

**ROI = (Revenue Attributable to Investment − Cost of Investment) / Cost of Investment × 100**

A $10,000 marketing campaign that generates $35,000 in attributable new revenue has a 250% ROI.

## What ROI Does Not Capture

Simple ROI is useful but incomplete. It doesn't account for:

**Taxes.** Capital gains taxes — both federal and state — reduce your after-tax return. Long-term gains (assets held > 1 year) are taxed at preferential rates (0%, 15%, or 20% federally depending on income). Short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income. ([SEC — Introduction to Investing](https://www.sec.gov/investor/pubs/begininvest.pdf))

**Inflation.** A 6% nominal return in a 3% inflation environment is only a 3% real return. Your purchasing power barely grew.

**Opportunity cost.** The true cost of any investment is not just what you put in — it's also what you could have earned on the next-best alternative.

**Risk.** ROI measures outcomes, not the probability distribution of outcomes. High ROI on a speculative position doesn't mean it was a smart decision — it may just mean you got lucky.

## ROI vs. IRR for Complex Cash Flows

When an investment involves multiple cash flows over time (rental income, dividend payments, staged capital contributions), **simple ROI and CAGR are imprecise** because they don't account for the timing of individual cash flows.

**Internal Rate of Return (IRR)** — the discount rate that makes the net present value of all cash flows equal to zero — is the more appropriate measure. It is standard in real estate investment analysis, private equity, and project finance.
