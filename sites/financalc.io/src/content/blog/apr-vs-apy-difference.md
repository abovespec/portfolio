When you open a savings account, the bank advertises APY. When you apply for a credit card, the lender shows APR. Both say "annual" and both involve interest rates, so it's easy to assume they're interchangeable. They aren't — and the difference explains why the same nominal interest rate produces different real-world results depending on how often it compounds.

## APR: Annual Percentage Rate

**APR** is the annual cost of borrowing money, expressed as a percentage. It includes the interest rate on the loan and, for most consumer loans under federal law, certain mandatory fees — but it does not account for compounding.

APR is primarily a **debt metric**. You'll see it on:
- Credit cards
- Mortgages
- Auto loans
- Personal loans
- Home equity lines of credit

A credit card with a 20% APR charges 20% per year on your balance. But if you carry a balance month to month, the credit card company doesn't charge you 20% once at year end — it divides the rate into daily increments and compounds it. The stated 20% APR, compounding daily, produces an effective annual cost that is slightly higher than 20%.

## APY: Annual Percentage Yield

**APY** is the actual return you earn on a deposit account after accounting for compounding. It takes the nominal interest rate and adjusts upward to reflect the effect of interest compounding on itself throughout the year.

APY is primarily a **savings metric**. You'll see it on:
- Savings accounts
- High-yield savings accounts
- Money market accounts
- Certificates of deposit (CDs)

When a bank advertises a savings account at 4.5% APY, that's the real return you earn over one year — including the compounding effect. No further adjustment needed.

## The Formula: Converting APR to APY

**APY = (1 + APR/n)^n − 1**

Where:
- **APR** = the stated annual rate (as a decimal)
- **n** = number of compounding periods per year

**Example 1:** A savings account with a 5% nominal rate, compounding monthly (n = 12):

APY = (1 + 0.05/12)^12 − 1  
APY = (1.004167)^12 − 1  
APY = 1.05116 − 1  
**APY = 5.116%**

The actual yield is 5.116%, not 5%. On a $10,000 balance, that's $511.60 earned instead of $500 — a difference of $11.60. On a larger balance or longer time horizon, the gap grows.

**Example 2:** Same nominal rate, compounding daily (n = 365):

APY = (1 + 0.05/365)^365 − 1  
APY = (1.0001370)^365 − 1  
**APY = 5.127%**

The difference between monthly and daily compounding is small (about 0.011 percentage points), but daily compounding is technically more favorable for the saver.

## Why the Distinction Matters in Practice

### For savings: Always compare APY

When comparing savings accounts, certificates of deposit, or money market accounts, always use APY — not the nominal rate or interest rate. Two accounts might advertise the same nominal rate but have different APYs depending on their compounding frequency. APY levels the playing field.

**Example:**
- Bank A: 4.80% nominal rate, compounded annually → APY = 4.80%
- Bank B: 4.75% nominal rate, compounded daily → APY ≈ 4.862%

Bank B's stated rate is lower, but its APY is higher because of daily compounding. If you only compared the nominal rates, you'd choose Bank A — and earn less.

### For borrowing: APR understates the real cost

When borrowing, APR tells you the stated cost, but it doesn't capture the full picture of compounding interest if you carry a balance. A credit card at 24% APR compounding daily has an effective annual rate (EAR) of about 26.97%.

For loans with fixed monthly payments (mortgages, auto loans), the APR is a reliable comparison metric because the amortization structure accounts for the payment schedule. Where APR misleads borrowers is revolving credit (credit cards) — the actual cost of carrying a balance is higher than the stated APR.

## APR vs. APY at a Glance

| Feature | APR | APY |
|---------|-----|-----|
| Accounts for compounding | No | Yes |
| Used for | Loans, debt | Savings, deposits |
| Legally required disclosure | Yes (TILA for loans) | Yes (TISA for deposits) |
| True cost/yield? | Understates (for compound debt) | Yes |
| Higher = better? | No (you pay it) | Yes (you earn it) |

## Credit Cards: Where APR Gets Confusing

Credit cards advertise APR, but interest compounds daily. Here's how it actually works:

1. Your 22% APR is divided by 365 to get a **daily periodic rate** of 0.0603%
2. Each day, that rate is applied to your current balance
3. At month end, the accumulated daily charges are added to your balance
4. Next month, interest is charged on the higher balance

If you have a $2,000 balance at 22% APR and make no payments:

- After 1 month: approximately $2,036.83 (at 22% APR ÷ 12 ≈ 1.833%/month)
- After 12 months: approximately $2,489.21 (compounding effect)
- The effective annual rate is about 24.36%, not 22%

This is why paying only the minimum on a credit card is so costly — and why eliminating credit card debt is almost always the highest-return financial move available.

## Mortgages: When APR Includes Fees

For mortgages, the federal Truth in Lending Act (TILA) requires lenders to disclose an APR that includes more than just the interest rate. Mortgage APR may also incorporate:

- Origination fees and discount points
- Mortgage broker fees
- Certain closing costs

This is why the APR on a mortgage quote is often higher than the interest rate. A loan quoted at 6.875% interest rate might carry a 7.1% APR once fees are included. When comparing mortgage offers, comparing APR is more apples-to-apples than comparing just the interest rate — though comparing closing cost structures separately is also important.

## How to Use This Knowledge

1. **Shopping for savings accounts:** Compare APY, not nominal rate. Higher APY = more money earned.
2. **Comparing loans:** Use APR as the primary comparison metric, but also ask about fees that might not be included.
3. **Evaluating credit card debt:** Recognize that your real annual cost of carrying a balance is higher than the stated APR due to daily compounding.
4. **Checking CD offers:** Insist on seeing APY before committing. A CD advertised at a high nominal rate might have lower APY than a competitor with a lower nominal rate and daily compounding.

## The Bottom Line

APR and APY measure related but distinct things. APR is the nominal annual rate without compounding — used primarily to describe the cost of debt. APY is the effective annual yield after compounding — the number that tells you what you'll actually earn on a savings account.

In short: when you're saving, look at APY. When you're borrowing, look at APR (and ask whether fees are included). In both cases, compounding frequency matters — and the longer your time horizon, the more those small differences add up.
