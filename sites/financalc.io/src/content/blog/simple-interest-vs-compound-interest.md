Two savings accounts, same balance, same interest rate, same bank. One uses simple interest, one uses compound interest. After 10 years, the compound account has noticeably more money. After 30 years, the difference is dramatic. Understanding why — and knowing which type you're dealing with in any given account or loan — is foundational financial literacy.

## Simple Interest: The Basics

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal. No matter how much time passes, the interest earned each period is based on the starting amount alone.

For more on this topic, see [*How Much House Can I Afford? A Practical Buyer's Guide*](/blog/how-much-house-can-i-afford).

**Formula: I = P × r × t**

Where:
- **I** = interest earned
- **P** = principal
- **r** = annual interest rate (as a decimal)
- **t** = time in years

**Example:** You lend a friend $2,000 at 5% simple interest for 3 years.

I = 2,000 × 0.05 × 3 = **$300**

Total returned: $2,000 + $300 = **$2,300**

Each year produces exactly $100 in interest. The amount never changes because the base (the original $2,000) never changes.

## Compound Interest: The Basics

Compound interest is calculated on the principal **plus** all previously accumulated interest. Each period, the balance grows, and the next period's interest is calculated on that larger balance.

**Formula: A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)**

Where:
- **A** = final amount
- **P** = principal
- **r** = annual interest rate (decimal)
- **n** = compounding periods per year
- **t** = time in years

**Example:** Same $2,000 at 5%, but compounded annually for 3 years.

A = 2,000 × (1 + 0.05/1)^(1×3)  
A = 2,000 × (1.05)^3  
A = 2,000 × 1.1576  
**A = $2,315.25**

The interest earned is $315.25 — compared to $300 under simple interest. After just 3 years, the difference is $15.25. That sounds small, but watch what happens over longer periods.

## Side-by-Side Comparison: $5,000 at 5%

| Years | Simple Interest Balance | Compound Interest Balance (Annual) | Difference |
|-------|------------------------|-------------------------------------|------------|
| 1 | $5,250 | $5,250 | $0 |
| 5 | $6,250 | $6,381 | $131 |
| 10 | $7,500 | $8,144 | $644 |
| 20 | $10,000 | $13,266 | $3,266 |
| 30 | $12,500 | $21,610 | $9,110 |
| 40 | $15,000 | $35,200 | $20,200 |

At Year 1, the two methods produce identical results. By Year 40, compound interest has generated more than twice as much total growth as simple interest on the same principal and rate. This divergence is not linear — it accelerates over time. That acceleration is the defining characteristic of compound growth.

## Where You Encounter Each Type

### Simple Interest in Practice

Simple interest appears most often in:

- **Short-term personal loans** — some lenders use simple interest on loans with fixed repayment schedules
- **Auto loans** — many car loans in the US use simple daily interest, where interest accrues each day on the outstanding principal balance
- **Some short-term notes** — informal or peer-to-peer lending arrangements often use simple interest for clarity
- **U.S. savings bonds** (certain types) — interest accrues on face value without compounding in some older bond structures

### Compound Interest in Practice

Compound interest is the standard for most financial products:

- **Savings accounts and high-yield savings accounts** — compound daily or monthly
- **Certificates of deposit (CDs)** — compound daily or monthly, paid at maturity or at intervals
- **Investment accounts** — returns reinvested in the portfolio compound over time
- **401(k) and IRA accounts** — growth compounds on the full balance
- **Credit cards** — compound daily on the outstanding balance (this works against you)
- **Most mortgages** — structured as amortizing loans but the interest calculation is compound
- **Student loans** — typically compound daily

## When Simple Interest Is Better for You

The answer depends on which side of the transaction you're on.

**As a borrower:** Simple interest is generally better. You pay interest only on the remaining principal, not on accumulated interest. As you pay down the loan, your interest charges shrink. This is why extra principal payments on a simple-interest auto loan save money immediately.

**As a lender or saver:** Simple interest is worse. You earn less than you would from compound interest over the same period.

## When Compound Interest Is Better for You

**As a saver or investor:** Compound interest is always better. Every dollar of accumulated interest becomes part of the base that earns future interest. The longer the time horizon, the more powerful this effect.

**As a borrower:** Compound interest works against you. Credit card debt at 24% APR compounds daily — meaning you're paying interest on your interest, and balances can grow quickly even without new charges.

## A Concrete Debt Example

$3,000 credit card balance at 20% APR, no payments made:

- **Simple interest:** After 3 years, total owed = $3,000 + ($3,000 × 0.20 × 3) = **$4,800**
- **Compound interest (daily):** After 3 years, total owed ≈ **$5,455**

The compound balance is $655 more. In reality, credit cards always use compound interest, which is why carrying a balance is so costly and why paying the full statement balance every month is one of the highest-return financial behaviors available to most people.

## The Continuous Compounding Edge Case

There's a theoretical limit to compounding: **continuous compounding**, where interest compounds infinitely many times per second. The formula becomes:

**A = P × e^(rt)**

Where e ≈ 2.71828 (Euler's number).

For $1,000 at 5% for 10 years: A = 1,000 × e^(0.5) = **$1,648.72**

Compare to annual compounding over the same period: $1,628.89. The difference between monthly compounding and continuous compounding is even smaller — continuous compounding is more of a mathematical concept than a practical distinction. No real financial product offers truly continuous compounding.

## How to Tell Which Type You Have

When reviewing any loan or savings product:

1. **Read the disclosures.** Truth in Lending Act (TILA) disclosures for loans and Truth in Savings Act disclosures for deposits are required by law and specify the compounding method.
2. **Look for APY.** If a savings product quotes an **Annual Percentage Yield (APY)**, the account uses compound interest. APY reflects the actual return after compounding.
3. **Look for APR vs. effective rate.** Loans show APR (nominal rate). If the effective annual rate is higher, compounding is at work.

## The Bottom Line

Simple interest is straightforward: you earn (or pay) a fixed amount based on the original principal each period. Compound interest snowballs: you earn (or owe) more each period because past interest joins the principal.

For more on this topic, see [*How Long Does It Take to Pay Off Debt? A Calculator-Based Guide*](/blog/how-long-to-pay-off-debt).

For savers and investors, compound interest is the goal. For borrowers, it's the cost to manage. The math is the same; what changes is which side of it you're on.

If you have high-interest compound debt, eliminating it should come before building savings — because the compound interest working against you in a 20% APR account almost certainly exceeds what compound interest can earn for you in a savings account paying 4-5%.
