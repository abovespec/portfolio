Most people have never thought about their age in weeks. But the number is striking when you see it—and in some contexts, weeks are the most meaningful unit available.

A 30-year-old has lived approximately **1,565 weeks**. That sounds like a lot, until you realize a career that starts at 22 and ends at 65 is only about 2,236 weeks long. Time expressed in weeks has a different texture than time expressed in years.

## How to Calculate Your Age in Weeks

Weeks are calculated from the total number of days lived, divided by 7.

**Step 1: Find your total days lived.**

Count calendar days from your date of birth to today. For the born-on date itself, the count starts at 0 (the day you were born is day 0; the next day is day 1).

**Step 2: Divide by 7.**

Total weeks = total days ÷ 7 (drop the remainder for complete weeks)

**Example:** Born September 14, 1992. Today is May 22, 2026.

Days from September 14, 1992 to May 22, 2026:

- September 14, 1992 to September 14, 2025 = 33 years
- 33 years includes leap years: 1996, 2000, 2004, 2008, 2012, 2016, 2020, 2024 = 8 leap years
- 33 × 365 + 8 = 12,053 days
- September 14, 2025 to May 22, 2026 = 249 days
- **Total: 12,302 days**

12,302 ÷ 7 = **1,757 complete weeks** (remainder 3 days)

## Week Milestones Worth Knowing

Some week counts carry a particular weight:

- **1,000 weeks** — roughly 19 years, 2 months. A milestone some people mark as a coming-of-age moment.
- **2,000 weeks** — roughly 38 years, 5 months. A mid-life marker that can prompt useful reflection.
- **3,000 weeks** — roughly 57 years, 8 months. Many people find this more visceral than turning 60.
- **4,000 weeks** — roughly 76 years, 11 months. The title of Oliver Burkeman's book on time, chosen because it represents a typical human lifespan.

That 4,000-week figure—about 77 years—is approximately the life expectancy in many developed countries. Thinking of life as 4,000 weeks rather than 77 years makes the finiteness more immediate. Burkeman's point was that recognizing this isn't morbid; it helps prioritize.

## Gestational Age: Where Weeks Matter Most

In obstetrics, everything is measured in weeks. Pregnancy is tracked from the first day of the last menstrual period, not conception:

- **Week 12:** End of first trimester; miscarriage risk drops significantly
- **Week 20:** Anatomy scan; sex can usually be determined
- **Week 24:** Viability threshold; survival outside the womb becomes possible with intensive care
- **Week 28:** Premature birth (before this, "very premature")
- **Week 32:** Lungs substantially developed
- **Week 37:** "Full term" begins (previously 38 weeks; revised in 2013)
- **Week 40:** Standard due date

Prematurity is also expressed in weeks after birth: a baby born at 28 weeks who is now 8 weeks old is said to be "36 weeks corrected gestational age"—still considered premature in terms of development.

## Postnatal Weeks for Newborns

Newborns are tracked in weeks for the first few months of life:

- Weeks 1–4: Newborn phase; sleep patterns, feeding, weight regain after birth
- Weeks 4–12: Social smiling, visual tracking, early vocalizations
- Week 6: Standard postnatal checkup in many countries
- Week 8: First vaccinations in the UK and US

A baby described as "10 weeks old" is more precisely located on the developmental curve than "2 months old," since months vary in length and the difference between 8 weeks and 10 weeks is meaningful at this stage.

## How Many Weeks in a Year? (And Why It's Not 52)

A common assumption: 1 year = 52 weeks. But 52 × 7 = 364, not 365. Each year has 52 weeks and 1 day (or 52 weeks and 2 days in a leap year).

This matters for age-in-weeks calculations because the error accumulates:

- Over 30 years: 30 extra days, or more than 4 additional weeks
- Over 70 years: approximately 10 additional weeks beyond 52 × 70

Any manual calculation that uses "52 weeks per year" will undercount by roughly a week per year. The correct method is to count calendar days and divide by 7.

## Week-Based Planning

Some people use their week-birthday as a deliberate planning anchor. Reviewing goals every 13 weeks (one quarter) or reflecting on milestones at 1,000-week intervals can create a different relationship with time than annual reviews alone.

A week-based perspective also makes progress more visible: habits form or break in weeks, not years. A 66-day habit formation estimate (the most replicated figure in behavioral research) is about 9 and a half weeks—a span that feels more actionable when stated that way.

## Calculating Weeks in Excel

```
=INT((TODAY()-A2)/7)
```

Where A2 contains your birthdate. `TODAY()-A2` returns the number of days, and `INT()` rounds down to complete weeks.

For remaining days after complete weeks:

```
=MOD(TODAY()-A2, 7)
```

Combined display:

```
=INT((TODAY()-A2)/7)&" weeks, "&MOD(TODAY()-A2,7)&" days"
```
