When someone says a business is "profitable," that word can mean very different things depending on which part of the income statement they are looking at. Gross margin and net margin are the two most commonly cited profitability measures — yet they measure fundamentally different things. Confusing them leads to poor pricing decisions, bad investment analysis, and misguided benchmarking.

This guide puts them side by side: same formulas, same examples, and a clear explanation of when each metric matters.

---

## The Core Difference in One Sentence

**Gross margin** measures how much profit remains after subtracting the direct cost of producing your product or service. **Net margin** measures how much profit remains after subtracting *every* cost the business incurs — production, overhead, debt, and taxes.

For more on this topic, see [*What Is Profit Margin? Definition, Formula, and Why It Matters*](/blog/what-is-profit-margin).

## Gross Profit Margin

### Formula

```
Gross Profit Margin (%) = ((Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue) × 100
```

**COGS (Cost of Goods Sold)** includes only the direct costs of production: raw materials, direct labor, manufacturing overhead. It excludes rent, sales salaries, marketing, administrative expenses, interest, and taxes.

### Example

A clothing brand sells $1,000,000 in merchandise. The fabric, manufacturing, and freight to the warehouse cost $420,000.

- Gross Profit = $1,000,000 − $420,000 = $580,000
- **Gross Margin = 58%**

For more on this topic, see [*How to Calculate Gross Profit Margin: Step-by-Step with Examples*](/blog/how-to-calculate-gross-profit-margin).

That 58 cents from every sales dollar is available to cover all other business expenses — and ideally leave a profit after doing so.

### What Gross Margin Reveals

- **Pricing power:** Can you charge enough above your production cost to run a business?
- **Production efficiency:** Are your direct costs well controlled?
- **Product mix:** High-margin products are subsidizing low-margin ones if the blended gross margin is declining.

Gross margin is the clearest signal of the underlying business model's viability. A company with a 15% gross margin cannot realistically achieve a healthy net margin after paying rent, staff, and taxes — there simply isn't enough room. A company with an 80% gross margin (common in software) has enormous flexibility.

## Net Profit Margin

### Formula

```
Net Profit Margin (%) = (Net Income ÷ Revenue) × 100
```

**Net Income** = Revenue − COGS − Operating Expenses − Interest − Taxes

### Example (continuing from above)

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Revenue | $1,000,000 |
| COGS | $420,000 |
| **Gross Profit** | **$580,000** |
| Salaries & Wages | $180,000 |
| Rent & Utilities | $60,000 |
| Marketing | $80,000 |
| Other Operating Expenses | $40,000 |
| **Operating Income** | **$220,000** |
| Interest Expense | $22,000 |
| Income Tax (21%) | $41,580 |
| **Net Income** | **$156,420** |

**Net Margin = $156,420 ÷ $1,000,000 = 15.6%**

The business started with a 58% gross margin and ended with a 15.6% net margin. The 42.4 percentage-point gap represents all operating overhead, debt service, and taxes.

### What Net Margin Reveals

- **Overall efficiency:** How well does the entire organization, not just production, convert revenue to profit?
- **Debt burden:** Companies with heavy debt loads will see a large spread between operating and net margin.
- **Tax efficiency:** Net margin reflects the after-tax reality shareholders actually receive.

Net margin is the number that matters to shareholders, lenders, and anyone evaluating whether a business is building long-term value.

## Side-by-Side Comparison

| | Gross Margin | Net Margin |
|---|---|---|
| **Measures** | Production efficiency | Total business profitability |
| **Costs included** | COGS only | COGS + operating expenses + interest + taxes |
| **Best for** | Pricing decisions, product analysis | Investor comparison, overall health |
| **Excludes** | All overhead, interest, taxes | Nothing — everything is included |
| **Formula** | (Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue | Net Income ÷ Revenue |
| **Typical range (varies widely)** | 20%–80% depending on industry | 5%–25% depending on industry |

## When to Use Each

**Use gross margin when:**
- Setting product prices or evaluating whether a new product line is viable
- Comparing production efficiency between periods or against competitors
- Diagnosing whether cost-of-goods inflation is squeezing profitability
- Analyzing businesses with very different overhead structures (comparing a bootstrapped startup to a heavily-staffed corporation, gross margin is more apples-to-apples)

**Use net margin when:**
- Evaluating a business as an investment
- Comparing the overall financial health of companies in the same industry
- Assessing whether a business can service its debt obligations
- Analyzing what portion of revenue actually flows to equity owners

---

## Why a High Gross Margin Doesn't Guarantee Profitability

This is the most important nuance. A business can have an excellent gross margin and still lose money — if its operating costs are too high relative to revenue.

**Example:** An early-stage SaaS company reports an 85% gross margin (nearly all revenue minus server and support costs). But it spends aggressively on sales and marketing — $2 in sales expense for every $1 of new recurring revenue. The result: a strongly negative net margin despite a world-class gross margin.

This is why analysts look at the full margin cascade (gross → operating → net) rather than any single metric in isolation. Gross margin tells you about the *unit economics*. Net margin tells you about the *business*.

## Industry Context

Both margins vary enormously by industry. According to NYU Stern's margin database, median values include:

| Industry | Gross Margin (approx.) | Net Margin (approx.) |
|----------|----------------------|---------------------|
| Software (internet) | ~70%–80% | ~15%–25% |
| Retail (general) | ~28%–35% | ~3%–6% |
| Restaurants | ~60%–65%* | ~3%–9% |
| Healthcare services | ~35%–45% | ~5%–10% |
| Manufacturing (general) | ~25%–35% | ~5%–10% |

*Restaurant "gross margin" is often calculated as revenue minus food cost only, which differs from traditional COGS treatment.

The only meaningful benchmark is your own industry's historical range — not a universal target.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Gross margin isolates production profitability by excluding all overhead, interest, and taxes.
- Net margin is the total profitability measure after every cost is accounted for.
- Both metrics are necessary — gross margin diagnoses unit economics; net margin judges the whole business.
- A high gross margin is necessary but not sufficient for a healthy net margin.
- Always benchmark against your specific industry, not a generic "good margin" target.
