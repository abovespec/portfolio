> **Disclaimer:** Card processing practices vary by bank and merchant. Details here describe common US practices; confirm specifics with your card issuer.

Paying by card at a restaurant involves a small payment system that most diners never think about — until they notice a pending charge that doesn't match what they spent, or wonder if their tip was processed correctly. Here's how it actually works.

## How Restaurant Card Tips Work

When you hand over your card at a restaurant, the process has two distinct stages:

**Stage 1 — Pre-authorization (the hold)**
When your server runs your card after you've paid but before writing in a tip, the restaurant places a temporary hold — called a pre-authorization — for the bill amount. Some processors add a buffer (commonly 15–20%) to account for the expected tip, so the pending charge you see may be larger than your subtotal.

**Stage 2 — Final settlement (the actual charge)**
Once the server collects the signed receipt with your tip written in, the restaurant submits the final amount — subtotal plus tip — for settlement. Your bank then posts this final charge and releases the pre-authorization hold. The process typically takes **1–3 business days**, though it can take longer on weekends or holidays.

## Why Your Pending Charge May Look Wrong

If you check your bank account immediately after a restaurant meal, you might see:

- A pending charge for just the subtotal (if the restaurant pre-authorizes for the exact bill)
- A pending charge that's 15–20% higher than the bill (if the processor added a tip buffer)
- A pending charge that later changes to a different (correct) final amount

This is normal. The pre-authorization amount is not the final charge. The settled transaction — which is the real charge — posts within a few days and reflects the exact tip you wrote in.

## What Happens When You Write In a Tip

The server or a manager manually enters or confirms the final amount when closing out your check. The amount you write on the tip line is what gets submitted to your bank. If you write "20%" instead of a dollar amount, someone at the restaurant calculates the dollar figure — which is a reason to always write the dollar amount yourself.

**Best practice:** Always write the dollar tip amount, calculate and write the total (subtotal + tip), and draw a line through any blank lines on the receipt to prevent alteration.

## The "Tip After Checkout" Model

Newer point-of-sale systems, especially at quick-service restaurants and some full-service establishments, ask for the tip digitally at checkout — either on a tablet or on a card reader — before you've finished paying. In this model:

- The tip is entered into the system at the time of transaction
- There is no handwritten receipt to alter
- The final total is submitted immediately with the tip included
- The pending charge reflects the complete amount from the start

This is increasingly common and eliminates the pre-auth gap.

## Can a Restaurant Change Your Tip Amount?

Theoretically yes — a merchant can submit a different amount than you authorized. This is rare, but it does happen (usually as errors, occasionally as fraud). How to protect yourself:

- **Keep your receipt copy** until the final charge posts to your account.
- **Check your statement** a few days after dining to confirm the posted amount matches your receipt.
- **If the amount is wrong**, contact your credit card issuer. Under federal Regulation Z and card network rules, you have the right to dispute unauthorized charges. Most banks make this easy through their app.
- **Document significant discrepancies** with a photo of your copy of the receipt before disputing.

## Cash Tips vs. Card Tips for the Server

From the server's standpoint:

**Cash tips** are received immediately at the end of the shift and are not subject to electronic processing delays.

**Card tips** are processed through payroll or the restaurant's tip payment system. In most restaurants, card tips are paid out the same day or next business day. Some restaurants pay card tips on a weekly payroll cycle.

Neither method disadvantages the server significantly in typical restaurants. Cash tips avoid any processing, but card tips are also tracked in the employer's system — which can matter for tip-out calculations and official income records.

## Tips and Tip Pooling

Many restaurants operate tip pools — card tips go into a shared pool that's distributed among servers, bussers, food runners, and sometimes bartenders. Some restaurants have house tip-out policies where a percentage of each server's card tips is contributed to the pool.

For more on this topic, see [*What Is the Average Tip Percentage at US Restaurants?*](/blog/average-tip-percentage).

This means your tip may not go entirely to the individual server. This is legal in most states (with rules about who can participate in pools) and is standard at many establishments. It doesn't change how much you should tip — the full standard amount still flows through the system to service workers.

## Quick Reference: The Card Tip Process

| Step | What Happens |
|---|---|
| Card is run | Pre-authorization hold placed (sometimes with buffer) |
| You write in tip | Dollar amount added to total on the physical receipt |
| Restaurant closes batch | Final amount submitted to processor |
| 1–3 business days later | Final charge posts; hold releases |
| Check your statement | Confirm posted amount matches your receipt |

## The Bottom Line

Tipping on a credit card works reliably in almost every case. The pre-authorization hold is temporary and the final settled charge will match what you wrote in. Keep your receipt copy until the charge posts, write the tip as a dollar amount rather than a percentage, and verify your statement for large or important meals. The system isn't perfect, but disputes are straightforward when you have documentation.
