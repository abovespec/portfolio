> **Disclaimer:** Tipping customs vary by venue and region. Amounts here reflect common US norms and are not mandatory fees.

Buffet dining sits in a middle zone: you're serving yourself from the food stations, but someone is still doing real work for your table. Knowing what that work is — and what it's worth — leads to a fair answer.

## What Buffet Staff Actually Do

At a typical buffet, the server assigned to your section:

- Clears plates as you return from the food line
- Brings and refills drinks
- Replaces used utensils and napkins
- Keeps the table clean between trips
- Handles any special requests (extra plates, to-go boxes, adjustments)

They are generally not bringing you food from the kitchen or taking a food order — those are the main labor tasks at a traditional restaurant. But they're still providing real tableside service, and in most states they are paid the tipped sub-minimum wage with the expectation that tips fill the gap.

## How Much to Tip at a Buffet

Because servers provide a subset of full-service labor, the standard tip is lower:

**Typical buffet tip: $1–2 per person at the table, or 10% of the total bill.**

The per-person flat rate is the most common approach. It's simple, scales naturally with table size, and directly reflects that a larger party means more plate clearing and drink refilling.

| Table Size | Suggested Tip |
|---|---|
| 1–2 people | $2–4 |
| 3–4 people | $4–8 |
| 5–6 people | $6–12 |
| Large group (8+) | $1–2 per person |

The 10% percentage approach works well for buffets with table service components — where the server checks in more frequently, handles drink orders with some complexity, or provides attentive service. Use whichever method produces the higher number when in doubt.

## When to Tip More Than the Standard

The basic $1–2/person is a floor, not a ceiling. Reasons to tip more:

- **Drinks service was attentive.** If your server refilled drinks proactively, kept glasses from going empty, and checked back regularly, this is full-service behavior that warrants closer to full-service tipping.
- **Long visit.** A 90-minute lunch occupying a server's section represents real lost opportunity for table turns. Compensate accordingly.
- **Children at the table.** Kids at buffets create extra cleanup. A larger tip acknowledges this.
- **You had many special requests.** Extra plates, allergy questions, multiple rounds of dessert containers — all real labor.
- **Exceptional cleanliness.** A server who proactively cleared plates after every trip and kept your table spotless did more work than average.

## Premium and Upscale Buffets

Some buffets — hotel brunch buffets, seafood buffets, high-end Sunday buffets — have more server involvement, including food presentation, carving station service, champagne service, and attentive drink refills. At these venues, a 15% tip is more appropriate because the service experience is closer to full-service dining.

| Venue Type | Suggested Tip |
|---|---|
| Casual buffet (Chinese, Indian, American) | $1–2/person or 10% |
| Mid-range buffet with drink service | $2/person or 10–12% |
| Upscale hotel or special occasion buffet | 15% |
| Buffet with carving station / tableside service | 15–18% |

## Who Else to Tip

At most buffets, your server is the only person you'll directly interact with. But at larger buffet establishments:

- **Carving station attendants:** A $1–2 tip if they carve for you specifically is a nice gesture, though not an expectation.
- **Beverage attendants at separate stations:** $1 if they're bringing drinks table-side from a separate station.
- **Coat check, valet:** Standard rates apply — $2–3 for coat check, $3–5 for valet.

Do not tip buffet cashiers or the hosts who seat you; there's no tipping convention for these roles.

## The Zero-Tip Question

Some diners leave nothing at buffets on the logic that self-service means no service. This is generally considered poor form in the US. Someone cleared your plates and refilled your drinks — likely at a tipped minimum wage — and $2–4 for a table of two is a minimal acknowledgment of that work.

The Emily Post Institute's guidance is that buffet tipping is expected, just at a lower rate than full-service dining.

## The Bottom Line

**$1–2 per person is the standard for casual buffet dining.** Ten percent works as a percentage approach, especially when you received attentive drink service. Tip at the higher end if service was genuinely attentive, if you stayed long, or if your table required more than average cleanup. Tipping zero is poor form regardless of the self-serve format — your server was still working your table on a tipped wage.
