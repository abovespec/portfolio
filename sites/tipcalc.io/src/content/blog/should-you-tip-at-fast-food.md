> **Disclaimer:** Tipping is voluntary. The guidance here reflects common US norms, not legal requirements.

Touchscreen payment terminals now prompt for tips at Subway, Chipotle, Starbucks, and fast casual chains across the country. The requests arrive at the end of every transaction, and many people feel unsure what to do. This article gives a direct answer.

## The Short Answer

You are **not obligated to tip at fast food and quick-service restaurants** where you order at a counter, no one brings food to your table, and service consists of handing you a bag or calling your name.

That doesn't mean you shouldn't — but the social expectation that exists at full-service restaurants does not apply here in the same way.

## Why the Prompt Appears Everywhere

The spread of tip prompts on tablets and POS systems (Square, Toast, Clover) is a design and business decision. Businesses noticed that a prompt — especially one that defaults to a suggested amount like 18% — generates tips that wouldn't otherwise happen. For businesses operating on thin margins, this is a free way to supplement worker pay without raising prices.

This is a legitimate business strategy, and the workers who receive those tips are better off for it. But the system has spread far beyond contexts where tips were ever traditional, creating widespread discomfort and what many describe as "tip fatigue."

## The Wage Difference That Changes the Equation

At traditional full-service restaurants, servers in most states are paid a tipped minimum wage — in some states as low as $2.13 per hour — with the legal expectation that tips bring them to the regular minimum wage. Tips are functionally their primary income.

For more on this topic, see [*Tipping Etiquette in the United States: A Service-by-Service Guide*](/blog/tipping-etiquette-united-states).

**Fast food and quick-service workers are generally paid the regular state or federal minimum wage, not the tipped sub-minimum.** In many states, that minimum wage has risen significantly — $15–17 per hour in California, New York, Washington, and others. Fast food workers in these states often earn more per hour before tips than tipped restaurant servers.

This doesn't mean fast food workers don't deserve more pay or that tips aren't welcome. It does mean the moral urgency is different. A 20% tip at McDonald's is not filling the same gap that a 20% tip fills at a table-service restaurant.

## When Tipping at Counter Service Makes Sense

There are genuine scenarios where tipping at counter-service makes sense:

| Situation | Tip Appropriate? |
|---|---|
| Someone brought food to your table | Yes — treat like a restaurant |
| Barista who made a complex drink order | $1–2 is reasonable |
| Staff who were notably kind or helpful | Tip if you feel moved to |
| Regular spot where you want to build goodwill | Tip to signal appreciation |
| Takeout from a fast-casual you ordered in-person | Optional; small round-up is fine |
| Strictly over-the-counter, no table service | Not obligated |

The Emily Post Institute's position: counter-service tips are "appreciated but not expected" in the same way full-service tips are.

## How to Handle the Prompt Without Awkwardness

Many people tip because they feel watched or pressured when the screen is turned toward them and the cashier is standing there. This is a real phenomenon and it's worth having a deliberate approach:

- **Decide in advance** what your policy is for these situations, so you're not making an in-the-moment judgment under social pressure.
- **The "No tip" or "Custom" button is designed to be pressed.** Businesses that add tip prompts accept that some customers will decline; they're playing percentages.
- **If you're a regular or the staff genuinely goes above, tip.** Tips at your regular coffee shop accumulate into a meaningful relationship over time.
- **Small rounds-up are not nothing.** If your total is $8.60 and you tap to round up to $9, that's $0.40 the worker gets. It's not a restaurant tip, but it's not meaningless.

## The Debate in Brief

Two coherent positions exist on this:

**"Tip prompts at fast food are manipulative and should be resisted."** Under this view, the proliferation of tip prompts is businesses offloading labor costs onto customers, and declining at fast food normalizes paying workers a living wage through wages rather than tips.

**"Workers benefit from tips regardless of the context, and if you can afford to tip, you should."** Under this view, the worker's circumstances — not the venue type — are what matters, and any extra income is meaningful.

Both positions have merit. The practical takeaway: **you have no social obligation to tip at quick-service restaurants the way you do at sit-down restaurants.** If you choose to tip, it's a genuine generosity; if you don't, you haven't violated a norm.

## The Bottom Line

Tipping at fast food and counter-service is optional, not expected. The workers who receive those tips are real people who benefit from them, but unlike servers at full-service restaurants, they aren't paid a sub-minimum wage with the legal assumption that tips cover the gap. Tip if the service was genuinely helpful, if you're a regular, or if you simply want to. Press "No Tip" without guilt when you don't.
