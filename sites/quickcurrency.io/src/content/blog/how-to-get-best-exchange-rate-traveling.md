Getting a good exchange rate when you travel doesn't require obsessive rate-watching or complex planning. It mostly comes down to knowing which exchange methods are efficient, which ones are expensive, and a few habits that consistently work in your favor.

## Start With the Mid-Market Rate

Before any trip, look up the mid-market rate between your home currency and your destination's currency using a [currency converter](/). This is the "true" exchange rate — the midpoint of what buyers and sellers are trading at in the global market.

Write it down or take a screenshot. You won't transact at this exact rate — every exchange method has a spread and possibly fees on top — but it gives you a reference point for evaluating what you're actually being offered. If you see a rate that's 10% worse than mid-market, you know to keep looking.

## The Methods Ranked by Typical Rate Quality

Not all exchange methods are equal. Here's how they tend to stack up, from best to worst:

**1. No-foreign-transaction-fee credit card** — For purchases you can pay by card, this is often the best rate available to travelers. Visa and Mastercard apply exchange rates very close to mid-market. With a fee-free card, the total cost above mid-market is typically under 1%.

**2. ATM withdrawal in local currency** — When you use an ATM abroad and decline Dynamic Currency Conversion (more on this below), your card network applies its exchange rate — usually close to mid-market. Your home bank may charge a flat fee per withdrawal ($3–$5 is common), which you can minimize by withdrawing larger amounts less frequently.

**3. Online currency exchange or specialist money transfer services** — For larger amounts or planning ahead, some services offer competitive rates with transparent fees. Rates vary, so comparing a few options is worthwhile for anything over $500.

**4. Your home bank before you travel** — Bank branches offer convenient exchange but typically apply a markup of 2–5% over mid-market plus a possible flat fee. Not the best rate, but reliably available and regulated. Better than exchanging at your destination's airport or hotel.

**5. Airport currency desks** — Convenient, but the rate is almost always significantly worse than alternatives — commonly 8–12% above mid-market. Use airport exchange only if you need a small amount of local cash immediately on arrival and have no other option.

**6. Hotel desks and tourist-area exchange booths** — These typically offer the worst rates in any given city. They serve a captive audience and price accordingly.

## Key Habits That Make a Consistent Difference

### Always Pay in Local Currency

Whether at a point-of-sale terminal, a restaurant, or an ATM, you will sometimes be offered the option to pay or withdraw in your home currency instead of the local one. This is called Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC), and it should almost always be declined.

When you accept DCC, the merchant or ATM operator applies their own exchange rate — which is typically 3–7% worse than what your card network would apply. They keep the difference. The "convenience" of seeing your home currency amount costs you real money.

At an ATM, the screen will ask something like "Would you like to be charged in USD instead of EUR?" Always choose the local currency. At a card terminal, if the receipt shows your home currency and you didn't choose that, you can often ask the merchant to run the transaction again in local currency.

### Minimize ATM Flat Fees by Withdrawing Strategically

If your bank charges a $5 flat fee per foreign ATM withdrawal, two $100 withdrawals cost $10 in flat fees — 5% of your cash. One $200 withdrawal costs $5 — 2.5%. One $400 withdrawal costs $5 — 1.25%.

Withdraw what you reasonably need for several days rather than popping to the ATM every time you need cash. Don't carry more than you're comfortable losing, but consolidating withdrawals reduces the flat-fee drag significantly.

### Avoid Exchanging More Than You Need

Currency exchanges are typically one-directional for travelers — the rate you get exchanging home currency to local currency is different from (and usually worse than) the rate for exchanging it back. If you return home with a significant amount of local currency, you'll lose something on both conversions.

Estimate what you'll actually spend in cash, and lean slightly on the lower side. You can usually find an ATM for more if needed. Coins especially are difficult to exchange and often not accepted at exchange counters at all.

### Use Cards for Large Purchases Where Possible

Hotels, car rentals, tour operators — these are typically the largest individual purchases on a trip. Paying by card (no foreign transaction fee card) at mid-market-adjacent rates beats exchanging a large amount of cash at a bank for these specific categories.

Cash makes more sense for small merchants, markets, taxis in some cities, and situations where cards aren't reliably accepted.

## When Airport Exchange Makes Sense

Airport rates are consistently bad, but that doesn't mean you should never use them. A few scenarios where it's reasonable:

**Arriving with zero local cash at a destination where cards are not widely accepted.** Some markets and transit systems in certain countries are essentially cash-only. Having the equivalent of $30–$50 in local currency when you land is worth paying a higher rate for, because the alternative is being unable to get a taxi or buy anything until you find a bank ATM.

**Departing with leftover foreign currency you won't be bringing back.** If you have coins or small notes that exchange counters at home won't accept, using them at the airport is better than discarding them.

For everything beyond immediate arrival needs, wait until you're in the city where ATM and bank options are more competitive.

## Before You Travel: A Simple Checklist

- Look up the mid-market rate and note it as a reference point using a [currency converter](/)
- Check whether your primary credit card charges foreign transaction fees — if so, consider a fee-free alternative
- Notify your bank that you'll be traveling internationally to prevent card blocks
- Identify whether your destination is primarily card-accepting or cash-dependent
- Check whether your bank reimburses foreign ATM fees or has partner ATMs at your destination
- Decide roughly how much cash you'll need and plan to withdraw it in one or two ATM visits

None of this is complicated. The gains come from avoiding the obviously expensive options (airport kiosks, DCC, high-fee cards) rather than optimizing obsessively. Most of the money saved on exchange rates comes from a few simple decisions made before you leave home.
