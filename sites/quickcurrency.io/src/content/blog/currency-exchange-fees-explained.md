Every time you convert one currency to another, someone takes a cut. The question is how much, in what form, and whether you can reduce it. The confusion comes from the fact that currency fees are rarely shown as a single clear number — they're spread across rate markups, flat charges, and percentage fees that stack on top of each other.

Here's how each type of fee works, how they compare, and what to watch for.

## The Fee Hidden in the Rate

Before looking at explicit fees, understand that the most significant cost of exchanging currency is often invisible: the spread between the mid-market rate and the rate you're actually offered.

For more on this topic, see [*What Is the Mid-Market Rate? Definition, Spread, and Why It Matters*](/blog/what-is-the-mid-market-rate).

The mid-market rate — the true exchange rate — is the midpoint between what buyers and sellers are willing to trade at in the global market. No retail customer gets this rate. Every exchange provider marks it up, and that markup is profit for them, cost for you.

A bank might quote you a rate that is 2–4% worse than the mid-market rate. An airport kiosk might be 8–12% worse. A dynamic currency conversion at a foreign ATM might be 5–7% worse.

Before worrying about any flat fee, check the rate against the mid-market rate using a [currency converter](/). If a provider claims "no fees" but the rate is 6% off, the fee is built into the rate — it's just less visible.

## Bank Exchange Fees

When you exchange currency at your bank — either at a branch or by ordering foreign notes online — you'll typically encounter:

**Rate markup**: The bank's offered rate will be worse than the mid-market rate by some margin. This varies by bank and by currency pair. Major currencies like EUR and GBP tend to have smaller markups; less common currencies may have wider ones.

**Transaction or order fee**: Many banks charge a flat fee for each foreign currency order, often in the range of $5–$15, though some waive this for account holders above a certain tier.

**Delivery fee**: If you order foreign currency to be shipped to your home, there may be a separate delivery charge.

For large amounts, the rate markup matters more than the flat fee. For small amounts, the flat fee can be the dominant cost. If you're picking up $200 worth of euros and paying a $10 flat fee, that's already a 5% surcharge before the rate markup.

## ATM Fees Abroad

Using an ATM in another country is often one of the most convenient ways to get local cash, but multiple fees can apply at once:

**Your home bank's foreign ATM fee**: Many banks charge a flat fee — often $3–$5 — for each withdrawal from a foreign ATM. Some banks and online-only accounts waive this entirely or reimburse it.

**The foreign ATM operator's fee**: The bank that owns the ATM overseas may charge its own fee, which gets added to your transaction. This varies widely by country and operator.

**Exchange rate markup**: The ATM uses an exchange rate set by either your card network (Visa or Mastercard) or the local bank. Card network rates are generally close to the mid-market rate — this is often the best rate mechanism available to travelers.

For more on this topic, see [*How to Get the Best Exchange Rate When Traveling*](/blog/how-to-get-best-exchange-rate-traveling).

**Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC)**: This is the trap. When an ATM asks if you want to pay in your home currency instead of the local currency, always decline. When you say yes, the ATM applies its own (typically very poor) exchange rate instead of letting your card network handle it. You pay for the "convenience" of seeing your home currency amount — usually an extra 3–7% on top of everything else.

A typical cost structure for an ATM withdrawal abroad: $3–$5 flat fee from your bank, possibly a small operator fee, and a rate that's close to mid-market if you decline DCC. That's a manageable total, especially for larger withdrawals.

## Credit Card Foreign Transaction Fees

When you pay by credit card in a foreign currency, two things happen: your card network converts the currency (usually at a rate close to mid-market), and your card issuer may charge a foreign transaction fee on top.

**Foreign transaction fee**: Many credit cards charge 1–3% of each foreign transaction. Cards marketed for travel typically waive this fee entirely. If your card has a 3% foreign transaction fee, every $1,000 you spend abroad costs an extra $30 in fees alone.

For more on this topic, see [*Travel Money Tips: How to Keep More of Your Cash When Traveling Abroad*](/blog/travel-money-tips).

**Card network conversion**: Visa and Mastercard apply their own exchange rates, which are generally close to the mid-market rate with a very small spread. American Express also converts currency but may apply a slightly larger spread depending on the situation.

**Dynamic Currency Conversion at point of sale**: Just as with ATMs, merchants abroad sometimes offer to charge your card in your home currency. The same rule applies: always choose to pay in the local currency. Your card network's rate will almost always be better than the merchant's.

A no-foreign-transaction-fee credit card used internationally typically beats most other exchange methods on rate alone — the card network rate is near mid-market, and with no fee on top, total cost is often under 1%.

## Comparison: Typical Total Cost by Method

To make this concrete, here's a rough range of what you might actually pay above the mid-market rate by method:

| Method | Typical total cost above mid-market |
|---|---|
| No-fee travel credit card | 0.5–1% (card network spread) |
| Home bank ATM withdrawal abroad (decline DCC) | 1–3% rate + flat fee |
| Bank branch exchange | 2–5% |
| Online currency exchange services | 0.5–2% |
| Airport/hotel currency desk | 5–12% |
| Dynamic Currency Conversion | 3–8% extra on top of other fees |

These are generalizations — actual rates vary by provider, country, and amount. But the ordering is fairly consistent.

## How to Minimize Fees in Practice

A few principles that hold across most situations:

**Know your baseline.** Before any trip, check the current mid-market rate using a [currency converter](/). That's your benchmark for evaluating any exchange rate you're offered.

**Use a travel-friendly card.** If you travel more than once or twice a year, a no-foreign-transaction-fee card typically pays for itself. The difference between a 3% fee card and a 0% fee card on $3,000 of travel spending is $90.

**Withdraw larger amounts less often.** If your bank charges a $5 flat fee per ATM withdrawal, one $200 withdrawal costs 2.5% in flat fees; one $400 withdrawal costs 1.25%. Fewer, larger withdrawals reduce the flat fee impact — though don't carry more cash than you're comfortable with.

**Avoid airport exchanges for large amounts.** Airport convenience comes at a significant rate cost. Exchange a small amount if you need immediate cash on arrival, then find a better option.

**Never accept DCC.** Always pay in local currency, at both ATMs and point-of-sale terminals. This is the single easiest way to avoid unnecessary fees.

Understanding these fee structures won't eliminate the cost of exchanging currency, but it will help you make informed choices — and avoid the more expensive options when better ones are available.
