You don't need a phone or an internet connection to do a currency conversion. The underlying calculation is straightforward multiplication — once you understand what an exchange rate actually represents, the arithmetic takes care of itself.

This guide walks through the formula, works through concrete examples, and gives you a few mental shortcuts you can use on the fly when you're standing at a market stall or checking a restaurant bill.

## The Core Formula

Every currency conversion comes down to one operation:

**Amount in foreign currency = Amount in your currency × Exchange rate**

Or flipped around:

**Amount in your currency = Amount in foreign currency ÷ Exchange rate**

That's it. The exchange rate is just a multiplier — it tells you how many units of the target currency you get for each unit of the source currency.

### Example 1: USD to EUR

Suppose the USD/EUR rate is 0.92. You have $150 and want to know how many euros that is.

```
150 × 0.92 = 138 EUR
```

You'd receive approximately 138 euros.

### Example 2: EUR to USD

Same rate. You're looking at a 45 EUR hotel charge and want to know the dollar cost.

```
45 ÷ 0.92 = 48.91 USD
```

When converting back to your home currency, you divide by the rate.

### Example 3: GBP to JPY

This is where people get confused — what if neither currency is the one in your rate quote? You just chain two conversions together. If you have GBP and want JPY, and you know GBP/USD = 1.27 and USD/JPY = 155:

```
Step 1: 100 GBP × 1.27 = 127 USD
Step 2: 127 USD × 155 = 19,685 JPY
```

Most [currency converter](/)-style lookups do this chain internally. Manually, you just work through it step by step.

## How to Find the Rate Before You Need It

The formula only works if you have the rate. Before a trip, look up the mid-market rate — the "true" exchange rate before any fees or markups — using a [currency converter](/). Write the rate down or take a screenshot. You don't need precision to the sixth decimal; two or three significant figures is enough for mental math.

For more on this topic, see [*What Is the Mid-Market Rate? Definition, Spread, and Why It Matters*](/blog/what-is-the-mid-market-rate).

For example: "roughly 0.92 EUR per dollar" or "about 155 yen per dollar."

## Mental Math Shortcuts

Exact math is easy with a calculator but harder on the spot. Here are practical shortcuts for common situations.

### The Percentage Method

If the rate is close to 1:1, think of the difference as a percentage adjustment.

USD/CAD is often around 1.36. That means 1 USD buys 1.36 CAD — Canadian prices are roughly 36% cheaper in dollar terms. If you see a price tag of CAD 100, subtract about 27% (because 100 ÷ 1.36 ≈ 73.5) to get the USD equivalent.

### The "Move the Decimal" Method

For currencies with large numbers relative to USD or EUR — such as Japanese yen (roughly 150 per USD) or Indonesian rupiah (roughly 16,000 per USD) — divide by a round number.

If USD/JPY is 150, divide any yen price by 150 to get dollars. A 3,000 yen dinner: 3,000 ÷ 150 = $20. For mental ease, divide by 100 first (3,000 ÷ 100 = 30), then divide that by 1.5 (30 ÷ 1.5 = 20). Two small steps instead of one harder one.

### The Fraction Approximation

Sometimes the rate conveniently approximates a simple fraction.

- USD/EUR at 0.90? Think "nine-tenths" — subtract 10% from the dollar amount.
- USD/GBP at 0.80? Think "four-fifths" — multiply by 0.8, or subtract 20%.
- USD/CHF at 1.00? Rough parity — prices are roughly the same number.

These aren't exact, but for a traveler deciding whether a restaurant looks reasonable, they're good enough.

## Accounting for Fees in Your Mental Estimate

The rate you calculate manually is the mid-market rate — what the market says a currency is worth. The rate you actually get at a bank, exchange bureau, or ATM will be worse than this by some margin. That margin pays for the service.

For more on this topic, see [*Bank Exchange Rate vs Market Rate: Why They're Different*](/blog/bank-exchange-rate-vs-market-rate).

A practical rule: assume you'll get 2–5% less than the mid-market rate at a decent bank or ATM, and potentially 8–12% less at an airport kiosk or hotel desk. When doing mental math, build in a buffer:

- If you calculated $150 worth of euros at the mid-market rate, expect to actually receive somewhere between $138 and $147 worth, depending on where you exchange.

This isn't a reason to stress over the math — it's a reason to have a rough baseline so you can recognize when a quoted rate is obviously unfavorable.

## When the Formula Trips People Up

Two common mistakes:

**Multiplying when you should divide (or vice versa).** Remember: if you're converting to a currency with a rate greater than 1 (like USD to JPY at 150), you multiply and get a bigger number. If you're converting back, you divide. When in doubt, ask yourself: "Should I end up with more units or fewer?" Yen are small units; you'll always have more yen than dollars.

**Using a stale rate.** Exchange rates move constantly. A rate from yesterday may be off by a meaningful amount for large transactions, less so for a dinner bill. For anything over a few hundred dollars, always check a current rate via a [currency converter](/).

## Practicing Before You Travel

The best time to build intuition for a foreign currency is before you land. Pick the major exchange rate for your destination and run through a few practice conversions:

- What does a $20 equivalent look like in local currency?
- What would a $100 hotel night cost in the local price tag?
- What's the local equivalent of a $5 coffee?

After a few repetitions, the math becomes automatic. You'll stop feeling lost when you see prices and start having a genuine feel for local value — which is exactly what you need to make confident decisions on the ground.
