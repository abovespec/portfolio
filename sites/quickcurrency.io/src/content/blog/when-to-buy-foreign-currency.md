One of the most common questions travelers ask is when they should exchange money before an international trip. Should you do it now, or wait? Exchange at home, or abroad? Convert the full amount in one go, or spread it out?

The honest answer is that perfectly timing a currency exchange requires predicting the future, which no one can reliably do. What you can do is make structurally sound decisions that consistently get you a good rate — without stress, without waiting for a "perfect" moment that may not come.

## The Wrong Way to Think About Timing

Many travelers try to time the market: wait for the rate to improve, watch news about the economy, or exchange only when the rate "looks good." This approach has a few problems.

First, currency markets incorporate enormous amounts of information continuously. Movements that look obvious in retrospect are not predictable in advance, even for professional traders. If you're waiting to exchange £500 of travel money for euros, you're not going to out-predict the market.

Second, the difference in rate between a good day and a bad day over a span of a few weeks is typically small relative to the cost of exchanging through an expensive method. A 1% rate improvement you wait for gets easily wiped out by choosing an airport kiosk over an ATM.

The method you use matters more than the timing. Get the method right first.

## When You Actually Need the Money

The most practical factor for timing isn't the rate — it's when you need the currency. A few principles:

**Don't exchange so early that you create unnecessary risk.** If you exchange 90 days before your trip, you're taking on 90 days of exchange rate movement for no real benefit. If the rate moves against you significantly, you've effectively overpaid. If it moves in your favor, you got lucky — but you might equally have waited. Exchanging 1–3 weeks before departure is a reasonable window for most travelers.

**Don't leave it so late that you pay a convenience premium.** Exchanging at the airport or hotel on day one because you ran out of time is expensive. Building in a week or two lets you use a better method — your bank, an ATM after arrival in the city, or a competitive online service.

**Use an ATM on arrival if you prefer not to pre-exchange.** For most destinations, withdrawing local currency from an ATM after you land is one of the best methods available. You get the card network's exchange rate (close to mid-market), and you only convert what you need. The only downside is having zero local cash during transit — which matters if you need a taxi or transit fare immediately on arrival and cards aren't accepted. In that case, a small pre-exchange for immediate arrival needs is sensible.

## Before the Trip vs. After Arrival

Here's a direct comparison of the two main approaches:

### Exchange Before You Leave

**Pros:**
- You arrive with cash in hand — no stress about finding an ATM
- You can shop around for a better rate when you're not rushed
- Useful for destinations where ATMs are scarce or unreliable

**Cons:**
- Bank branch rates are typically worse than ATM rates — 2–4% over mid-market is common
- You may end up with more cash than you need
- You're exposed to rate movements between exchange and travel

**Best for:** Destinations where card acceptance is limited, first-time visitors to unfamiliar countries, or travelers going somewhere with ATM reliability issues.

### Use ATMs After Arrival

**Pros:**
- Card network rates are close to mid-market — generally better than bank branch exchanges
- You only convert what you need, reducing leftover currency
- Available in almost every country

**Cons:**
- Need a small amount of local cash for immediate arrival needs (transit, taxi) if cards aren't accepted
- Flat fees per withdrawal add up if you withdraw frequently in small amounts
- Your home bank may block your card or charge high fees — check before traveling

**Best for:** Most destinations in Europe, Asia, and the Americas with good ATM infrastructure; travelers with low or no-fee debit cards.

## How to Handle Rate Timing If You Want to Try

If you have flexibility — say, a trip three months away and you need to convert a significant amount — it's reasonable to pay some attention to rates. But keep the approach simple:

**Check the current rate against recent history.** Use a [currency converter](/) to see the current mid-market rate, and look at a 90-day chart if one is available. Is the rate near the high end or low end of its recent range? If your home currency is currently weak relative to recent months, waiting for a rebound might be reasonable. If it's near a multi-month high, converting now locks in a favorable position.

**Set a rate alert, not a watching habit.** Many currency apps let you set an alert when a rate crosses a threshold. Decide what rate would make you happy — say, 5% better than today — and set an alert for that level. If it triggers before your trip, convert. If it doesn't, convert anyway a week or two before departure. This removes the psychological burden of watching rates daily.

**Split large amounts across two conversions.** If you're converting a large sum — for a long trip, relocation, or property purchase abroad — converting half now and half in a few weeks is a simple way to average out rate timing without trying to pick a single perfect moment. This approach is sometimes called "dollar-cost averaging" in investment contexts; it applies equally to currency.

## What to Do in the Last Week Before Departure

With a week to go, stop worrying about the rate and focus on logistics:

- Check whether your bank and cards are set up for international use (notify your bank if required)
- Confirm your card's foreign ATM fee structure
- Decide whether your destination is card-friendly or cash-dependent
- If cash-dependent, exchange through a competitive channel — your bank's online order, a specialist currency service, or a local exchange bureau with a competitive rate
- Check that you have a small amount of local currency for immediate arrival needs if your destination doesn't have easily accessible ATMs in the arrival area

The travelers who save the most on currency exchange are usually not the ones who timed the market — they're the ones who avoided expensive convenience options, used the right card, and withdrew cash from in-city ATMs rather than airport kiosks. Getting those structural choices right is almost always worth more than rate timing.
