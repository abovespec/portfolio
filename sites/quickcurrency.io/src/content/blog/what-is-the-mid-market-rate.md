If you've looked up a currency exchange rate online and then gone to a bank or exchange bureau and found a different rate, you've encountered the gap between the mid-market rate and the retail rate. Understanding this gap — what it is, where it comes from, and how large it typically is — is one of the most practically useful things you can know about foreign exchange.

## The Definition

The mid-market rate (also called the interbank rate or the spot rate) is the midpoint between the buy price and the sell price of a currency pair in the global foreign exchange market at any given moment.

In other words, if EUR/USD has a buy price of 1.0850 and a sell price of 1.0852, the mid-market rate is 1.0851 — the middle of the two.

When you look up exchange rates on a financial news site, use a [currency converter](/), or see rates quoted in a broad economic context, you are almost always seeing the mid-market rate. It's the "real" exchange rate in the sense that it reflects what the market says a currency is worth, without any service provider's markup applied.

## Where It Comes From

The foreign exchange market — often called forex or FX — is a global, decentralized market where currencies are traded continuously during business hours across time zones. It's the largest financial market in the world by daily trading volume.

Within this market, the most active participants are large institutions: major banks, central banks, institutional investors, hedge funds, and large corporations managing international cash flows. These participants trade with each other at very tight spreads — fractions of a cent — and the aggregate of their activity produces the prevailing market rate at any moment.

For more on this topic, see [*Travel Money Tips: How to Keep More of Your Cash When Traveling Abroad*](/blog/travel-money-tips).

This interbank rate is what the mid-market rate reflects. It's not published by a single authority; it emerges from continuous trading activity. Financial data providers collect quotes from this market and calculate the midpoint, which is what you see on financial sites and in converter tools.

## The Spread Explained

The spread is the difference between the price at which a currency can be bought and the price at which it can be sold. In the interbank market, spreads are extremely tight for major currency pairs — sometimes less than a tenth of a cent on EUR/USD.

By the time a transaction reaches a retail customer, that spread has widened considerably. Here's why: every entity in the chain between the interbank market and you — your bank, the exchange bureau, the ATM operator — needs to make a margin on the transaction. They do this by quoting you a rate worse than the mid-market rate. If EUR/USD mid-market is 1.0851:

- A bank might buy euros from you at 1.0600 (they pay you less)
- A bank might sell euros to you at 1.1100 (you pay more)

The difference between 1.0600 and 1.1100 around a mid-market of 1.0851 is the retail spread. This is profit for the bank and cost for you, even if no explicit "fee" is listed on the transaction.

## Why It Matters Practically

The mid-market rate matters because it's your benchmark. Without it, you have no way to evaluate whether the rate you're being offered is reasonable or expensive.

Consider two scenarios for exchanging $1,000 to euros:

- Mid-market rate: 0.9200 EUR/USD → $1,000 = 920 EUR
- Bank A offers: 0.8900 EUR/USD → $1,000 = 890 EUR (3.3% worse than mid-market)
- Exchange kiosk offers: 0.8400 EUR/USD → $1,000 = 840 EUR (8.7% worse than mid-market)

Without the mid-market rate as a reference, both Bank A and the kiosk might look acceptable. With it, you can see that the kiosk is costing you an extra €50 on a $1,000 exchange — a meaningful difference for a travel budget.

## The "No Fees" Claim

Some exchange services advertise "no fees" or "zero commission." This is technically possible while still being more expensive than alternatives — if the fee is entirely embedded in the rate.

A service that charges 0% explicit fees but offers a rate 5% worse than mid-market is more expensive than a service that charges an explicit 0.5% fee on a rate 0.3% worse than mid-market. The only way to compare is to calculate what you'd actually receive for a given amount and compare that to what the mid-market rate would give you.

The all-in comparison is: how many units of the foreign currency do I receive for my home currency amount, versus how many would I receive at the mid-market rate? The difference, expressed as a percentage, is your total cost.

## How the Mid-Market Rate Changes

The mid-market rate is not fixed. It moves continuously during trading hours in response to:

- **Economic data releases**: Inflation reports, employment figures, and GDP data cause immediate rate moves.
- **Central bank decisions and statements**: Interest rate decisions and forward guidance from central banks like the Federal Reserve, ECB, and Bank of England move rates significantly.
- **Geopolitical events**: Elections, trade policy changes, and geopolitical instability all affect currency values.
- **Market sentiment**: Expectations about future economic conditions are sometimes a bigger driver than current data.

For most travelers and casual currency exchanges, day-to-day rate movements are noise. A 0.5% rate move on a $500 exchange is $2.50 — less than what you'd lose choosing an airport kiosk over an ATM. Focus on the method of exchange first; optimize the timing only if you have flexibility and are dealing with large amounts.

## Getting the Rate You Actually Want

No retail customer transacts at the mid-market rate. But you can get close to it.

Card networks (Visa, Mastercard) apply rates very close to mid-market when you use a credit or debit card internationally. Some specialist currency services and multi-currency accounts also offer rates within a fraction of a percent of mid-market, with a transparent flat fee on top.

Understanding the mid-market rate and checking it before any significant exchange — with a [currency converter](/) — puts you in a position to evaluate any offer you receive. That alone is usually worth more than any optimization of timing or method.
