When you apply for a mortgage, your lender will likely offer you a choice: pay a higher upfront cost in exchange for a lower interest rate, or keep closing costs lower and accept a higher rate. The upfront cost is called **discount points** (also simply "mortgage points" or "buying down the rate"). Each point costs 1% of the loan amount and typically reduces the interest rate by a fixed increment.

Whether buying points is a good financial decision depends entirely on one number: your break-even point.

## What Is One Mortgage Point?

One point = 1% of the loan amount, paid at closing.

On a $400,000 loan, one point costs **$4,000**.

In exchange, the lender reduces your interest rate — typically by around 0.25%, though the exact reduction per point varies by lender, market conditions, and loan type. Your Loan Estimate will show the specific rate reduction being offered.

You can buy fractional points (0.5 points, 1.5 points, etc.) or in some cases up to 3–4 points, depending on the lender.

## The Break-Even Calculation

The break-even point is the number of months it takes for your monthly payment savings to equal the upfront cost of the points.

**Formula:**
> Break-Even Months = Upfront Cost of Points ÷ Monthly Payment Savings

### Example: $400,000 Loan

**Without points:** 6.75% rate → monthly payment = **$2,594**  
**With 1 point ($4,000):** 6.50% rate → monthly payment = **$2,528**  
**Monthly savings:** $2,594 − $2,528 = **$66**

> Break-even = $4,000 ÷ $66 = **60.6 months → approximately 5 years**

If you stay in the home (and keep this mortgage) for more than 5 years, you come out ahead. If you sell or refinance before Month 60, you paid $4,000 for savings you never fully realized.

### Extended example: 2 points

**With 2 points ($8,000):** 6.25% rate → monthly payment = **$2,463**  
**Monthly savings vs. no points:** $2,594 − $2,463 = **$131**

> Break-even = $8,000 ÷ $131 = **61 months → approximately 5 years 1 month**

Interestingly, two points and one point have similar break-even timelines in this example. After the break-even, two points produce larger monthly savings ($131 vs. $66) and a larger total benefit over time.

### Total interest savings over 30 years

If you keep the loan to maturity:

- 1 point: total interest saved ≈ **$23,760** ($66 × 360 months) minus $4,000 upfront = **net benefit ~$19,760**
- 2 points: total interest saved ≈ **$47,160** ($131 × 360) minus $8,000 = **net benefit ~$39,160**

## Tax Considerations

In many cases, mortgage points paid on a primary residence purchase are deductible in the year paid (subject to IRS rules and income limitations). Points paid on a refinance must typically be amortized over the loan term rather than deducted in full upfront.

For more on this topic, see [*15-Year vs. 30-Year Mortgage: Amortization Differences and Trade-offs*](/blog/15-year-vs-30-year-mortgage).

This tax treatment, where applicable, improves the effective break-even point — the real after-tax cost of the points is lower than the nominal cost. Consult a tax advisor to determine whether you qualify and what deduction applies to your situation.

## When Buying Points Makes Sense

**You plan to stay long-term.** The longer you keep the mortgage, the more monthly savings accumulate. Buyers who plan to stay in a home for 10+ years often benefit significantly from buying down the rate.

**You are rate-sensitive.** Borrowers on tighter monthly budgets may value the certainty of a lower payment, even if the break-even is 5–6 years.

**You have surplus cash at closing.** Points should be purchased with liquid savings, not by stretching to cover them. If buying points depletes your emergency fund or down payment, the tradeoff is unfavorable.

**Rates are high and you do not expect to refinance soon.** In a high-rate environment, buying points locks in a lower rate. If rates fall significantly in the next few years, however, you may refinance anyway — and unspent point value goes with it.

**The rate reduction per point is favorable.** The typical reduction is around 0.25% per point, but some lenders offer 0.375% or even 0.5% per point. Always compare the actual rate-reduction-per-dollar being offered.

## When Buying Points Does NOT Make Sense

**You plan to sell within a few years.** The break-even period for most point purchases is 4–6 years. If you expect to move, divorce the property, or downsize before then, points are money left behind.

**You expect to refinance soon.** If you think rates will fall significantly in the next 1–3 years and you will refinance to capture the lower market rate, the points paid on the original loan are largely wasted. You do not carry points from one loan to a refinance.

**Cash is tight.** Points paid at closing increase your upfront costs. If you are already stretching for the down payment and closing costs, preserving cash may be more important than a lower rate.

**The per-point reduction is small.** If the lender is offering only 0.125% per point, the break-even stretches to 10+ years, dramatically reducing the range of scenarios where points pay off.

For more on this topic, see [*How to Pay Off a 30-Year Mortgage in 15 Years*](/blog/how-to-pay-off-30-year-mortgage-early).

## How to Compare Offers Across Lenders

When comparing loan offers, use the Loan Estimate (which federal law requires lenders to provide within 3 business days of your application) to compare:

1. The rate offered at par (zero points)
2. The rate offered with 1 point
3. The rate offered with 2 points
4. The rate on a "no-cost" loan (where the lender pays closing costs in exchange for a higher rate — this is the inverse of points)

Run the break-even calculation on each option using your realistic expected hold period. The answer often differs by lender, because the rate reduction per point varies.

## Lender Credits: The Inverse of Points

The same math works in reverse. Instead of paying points to lower your rate, you can accept **lender credits** — the lender pays some of your closing costs in exchange for a higher interest rate. This increases your monthly payment but reduces cash needed at closing.

For more on this topic, see [*How Mortgage Amortization Works: Principal, Interest, and Your Monthly Payment*](/blog/how-amortization-works).

Lender credits make sense when:
- You are short on cash
- You plan to sell or refinance within 3–4 years (before the higher rate cost exceeds the closing cost savings)

The break-even logic is identical: divide the closing cost savings by the monthly payment increase to find how many months it takes for the higher rate to cost more than what you saved upfront.

## A Decision Framework

| Situation | Consider |
|-----------|---------|
| Staying 10+ years, stable rate environment | Buy 1–2 points |
| Staying 5–7 years | Run the break-even; borderline case |
| Staying under 5 years | Avoid points; consider lender credits |
| Expecting to refinance in 2–3 years | Avoid points |
| Cash-constrained at closing | Avoid points or consider lender credits |
| Cash-rich, long-term mindset | Points likely worth it |

The break-even calculation is not complex, and the Loan Estimate gives you all the inputs you need. Before closing, run the numbers for your specific loan — including your realistic hold period — using our [amortization calculator](/) to compare the total interest paid under each rate scenario.

Points are neither inherently good nor bad. They are a prepayment of interest in exchange for a rate reduction. Whether that trade is favorable is purely a function of how long you keep the mortgage.
