Every fixed-rate mortgage comes with an amortization schedule — a table that maps out every single payment from Month 1 to the final payoff. Most borrowers glance at the monthly payment figure, sign, and never look at the table again. That is a missed opportunity. The schedule is one of the most powerful documents in your mortgage file, and once you understand each column, you can use it to reduce your total interest cost and track your equity month by month.

This guide walks through every column in detail, using a single example loan so all the numbers connect.

## The Example Loan

Throughout this article we will use:

- **Loan amount:** $300,000
- **Interest rate:** 6.5% fixed
- **Term:** 30 years (360 payments)
- **Monthly payment:** $1,896.20

*These figures are illustrative. Actual rates vary with market conditions, credit profile, loan type, and lender.*

## Column 1: Payment Number

The payment number is a simple sequential counter — 1 through 360 for a 30-year mortgage. It tells you exactly where you are in the loan's life. Payment 1 is the first month after closing; payment 360 clears the balance to zero.

Why it matters: when you look up your current payment number, you can instantly see your remaining balance in the "Balance" column of the same row — without waiting for a statement.

## Column 2: Payment Amount

For a fixed-rate mortgage, this number never changes. Every row shows the same dollar amount: **$1,896.20** in our example.

The payment is calculated at closing using the standard mortgage formula, which accounts for the loan amount, the monthly interest rate, and the number of payments. Once set, it stays constant even as the split between interest and principal shifts dramatically over time.

Adjustable-rate mortgages (ARMs) recalculate this figure periodically, which is one reason ARMs can be harder to plan around.

## Column 3: Interest Paid

This column shows how much of that month's payment goes to the lender as the cost of borrowing. It is calculated directly from the remaining balance:

> **Interest = Remaining Balance × (Annual Rate ÷ 12)**

For Month 1:
> $300,000 × (0.065 ÷ 12) = $300,000 × 0.005417 = **$1,625.00**

For Month 2, the balance has dropped to $299,728.80, so:
> $299,728.80 × 0.005417 = **$1,623.53**

The interest charge falls by a small amount each month as the balance declines. Early in the loan, this column dominates. In Month 1, $1,625.00 of the $1,896.20 payment — **85.7%** — is pure interest.

## Column 4: Principal Paid

This is the amount that actually reduces what you owe. It is simply the remainder after interest is subtracted from the total payment:

> **Principal = Payment − Interest**

Month 1: $1,896.20 − $1,625.00 = **$271.20**

Month 2: $1,896.20 − $1,623.53 = **$272.67**

The principal column starts small and grows every month. By Month 180 (Year 15), approximately $730 of each payment goes to principal. By Month 300 (Year 25), it's over $1,200. In the final months, nearly the entire payment is principal.

This gradual shift — from mostly interest to mostly principal — is the core mechanic of amortization.

## Column 5: Remaining Balance

The remaining balance column shows exactly how much you still owe after each payment is applied:

> **New Balance = Previous Balance − Principal Paid**

| Payment | Total Payment | Interest | Principal | Balance |
|---------|--------------|----------|-----------|---------|
| 1 | $1,896.20 | $1,625.00 | $271.20 | $299,728.80 |
| 2 | $1,896.20 | $1,623.53 | $272.67 | $299,456.13 |
| 3 | $1,896.20 | $1,622.05 | $274.15 | $299,181.98 |
| 12 | $1,896.20 | $1,608.56 | $287.64 | $297,278.74 |

After one full year of payments, you have paid **$22,754.40** and your balance has fallen from $300,000 to approximately $297,279 — a reduction of just **$2,721**. The remaining $20,033 went to interest.

This is why understanding the schedule matters: the numbers can be jarring, but they reflect the mathematical reality of front-loaded interest that comes with every standard mortgage.

## The Cumulative Columns (When Present)

Many amortization tools — including the [amortization calculator](/) — add two optional columns:

- **Cumulative Interest:** total interest paid from Month 1 through the current row
- **Cumulative Principal:** total principal paid from Month 1 through the current row

These columns answer the question: "How much have I spent in total, and how much of that actually reduced my debt?" At any given payment number, adding the two cumulative figures should equal your total payments made to date.

At Year 15 (payment 180) on our $300,000 loan:
- Cumulative interest paid: approximately **$160,400**
- Cumulative principal paid: approximately **$21,700**
- Remaining balance: approximately **$278,300**

## Using the Schedule to Make Decisions

### Spotting your equity position

Divide the remaining balance at any row by your home's estimated current value. That ratio is your loan-to-value (LTV). When LTV falls below 80%, you can typically request cancellation of private mortgage insurance (PMI), which can save hundreds of dollars per month.

### Verifying your servicer's statement

Every month your servicer should report the same interest and principal split the schedule predicts. If the numbers differ consistently, contact your servicer in writing to request an explanation. The CFPB maintains resources on mortgage servicing errors.

### Modeling extra payments

Adding even a modest extra principal payment each month compresses the schedule significantly. Adding $200/month extra to our example loan ($300,000 at 6.5%/30 years) saves approximately $80,000 in total interest and pays off the loan about 7 years early. Use the [amortization calculator](/) to model any extra-payment scenario with a revised schedule.

### Identifying the crossover point

The crossover point is the payment number where principal first exceeds interest in a single payment. On a 30-year, 6.5% loan, this occurs around Month 244 — roughly Year 20. Before that point, more than half of every dollar goes to interest; after it, more than half goes to equity.

## Quick Reference: What Each Column Tells You

| Column | Question It Answers |
|--------|-------------------|
| Payment # | Where am I in the loan's life? |
| Payment Amount | How much is due this month? |
| Interest Paid | What is the cost of borrowing this month? |
| Principal Paid | How much equity did I build this month? |
| Remaining Balance | What do I still owe right now? |
| Cumulative Interest | How much have I paid to borrow so far? |
| Cumulative Principal | How much equity have I built in total? |

An amortization schedule is not complicated once you see the logic connecting each row to the last. The same three-step calculation — interest from balance, principal from remainder, new balance from subtraction — repeats 360 times. What changes is the proportion. Tracking those proportions is how disciplined borrowers stay informed and find opportunities to save.
