If you have built equity in your home, two products let you tap it without selling: a home equity loan and a home equity line of credit (HELOC). Both use your home as collateral and both result in a second lien on the property. Beyond those similarities, they work very differently — and choosing the wrong one for your situation can cost you money or leave you exposed to risk.

## What Is a Home Equity Loan?

A home equity loan is a fixed-amount, fixed-rate loan secured by your home equity. You receive the entire loan proceeds in a single lump sum at closing, then repay it in equal monthly installments over a fixed term — typically 5 to 30 years.

**Key characteristics:**
- Fixed interest rate for the life of the loan
- Fixed monthly payment that never changes
- Full amount disbursed at closing
- Fully amortizing (each payment covers interest and principal)
- Predictable payoff date

Because the rate and payment are fixed, a home equity loan behaves like a conventional mortgage: you know exactly what you owe and exactly when it ends. Use the [amortization calculator](/) to model what a home equity loan's amortization schedule would look like.

## What Is a HELOC?

A HELOC is a revolving line of credit — more like a credit card than a loan. The lender approves a maximum credit limit based on your equity, and you draw funds as needed over a **draw period** (typically 10 years). You repay what you borrow and can re-borrow up to the limit again.

**Key characteristics:**
- Variable interest rate (usually tied to the prime rate plus a margin)
- Draw period: borrow freely, pay interest-only or minimum payments
- Repayment period: after the draw period ends, the balance amortizes over the remaining term (often 20 years)
- Payment fluctuates with interest rates and outstanding balance
- Flexible — borrow only what you need, when you need it

The CFPB notes that HELOCs typically carry variable rates that can increase significantly over the life of the line, which means monthly payments can rise considerably during the repayment period.

## Side-by-Side Comparison

| Feature | Home Equity Loan | HELOC |
|---------|-----------------|-------|
| Interest rate | Fixed | Variable (usually) |
| Disbursement | Lump sum at closing | Draw as needed |
| Monthly payment | Fixed | Variable |
| Draw flexibility | None — one-time | Yes — borrow, repay, re-borrow |
| Best for | Known, one-time expense | Ongoing or uncertain expenses |
| Rate risk | None after closing | Rate increases over time |
| Closing costs | Yes (typically) | Yes, often lower or zero |
| Tax deductibility | Interest may be deductible if used for home improvement* | Same* |

*Consult a tax advisor regarding deductibility. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 limited deductibility to proceeds used to "buy, build, or substantially improve" the home securing the loan.*

## A Worked Example

**Home value:** $450,000  
**Mortgage balance:** $280,000  
**Equity:** $170,000  
**Typical maximum borrowing (80% LTV):** $450,000 × 0.80 − $280,000 = **$80,000**

Most lenders cap combined loan-to-value at 80–85%, meaning you can typically access up to $80,000 in this scenario.

### Home Equity Loan Scenario

You borrow $50,000 at 8.0% fixed for 10 years.

Monthly payment: approximately **$607**  
Total interest over 10 years: approximately **$22,800**  
Payoff: exactly 120 months from closing

### HELOC Scenario

You open a $50,000 HELOC at prime + 1% (assume 8.5% initially).

Draw period (Year 1–10): You draw $30,000 for a renovation. Interest-only payments on $30,000 at 8.5% = approximately **$212/month**.

Repayment period (Year 11–30): The $30,000 balance amortizes. But if rates rise to 10.5% by Year 11, your monthly payment could be **$300+** — on a smaller balance than the home equity loan, but with less certainty.

## When a Home Equity Loan Makes More Sense

Choose a home equity loan when:

- **The expense is defined and one-time.** A kitchen remodel with a firm contractor bid, a debt consolidation payoff, or a college tuition payment is a known quantity. A lump sum at a fixed rate is efficient and predictable.
- **You need budget certainty.** Fixed payments eliminate the risk of rate increases disrupting your monthly budget.
- **You want a definite payoff date.** A home equity loan terminates cleanly after the final payment. There is no revolving balance temptation.
- **You are risk-averse about variable rates.** If you are on a fixed income or tight budget, variable-rate exposure is a real risk.

## When a HELOC Makes More Sense

Choose a HELOC when:

- **The expense is ongoing or uncertain.** A multi-phase home renovation, a college student with expenses spread over 4 years, or a small business owner needing flexible access to capital benefits from a draw-as-needed structure. You pay interest only on what you use.
- **You want an emergency liquidity backstop.** Many homeowners open a HELOC and never draw on it. Having a line available in case of job loss or medical emergency provides security without ongoing interest cost.
- **You expect to repay quickly.** If you can pay down draws fast, the variable rate risk is limited.
- **Current rates are favorable and you believe they may drop.** Variable rates can decrease as well as increase, though planning around rate decreases is speculative.

## The Risks Both Products Share

Because both loans use your home as collateral, **the risk is the same: failure to repay can result in foreclosure**. This is the most important distinction from unsecured debt. A default on a credit card damages your credit; a default on a home equity loan or HELOC can cost you your home.

Additional shared risks:
- **Overleveraging:** Borrowing against equity reduces the financial buffer between your home's value and what you owe. A decline in property values can leave you underwater (owing more than the home is worth).
- **Closing costs and fees:** Both products typically involve appraisal, origination, and recording fees. A small loan may not be worth the overhead.
- **Impact on primary mortgage refinancing:** A second lien complicates future refinancing of your primary mortgage. The second-lien holder must agree to be subordinated.

## Questions to Ask Before Choosing

1. Is my expense a one-time lump sum or ongoing? (Lump sum → home equity loan; ongoing → HELOC)
2. How sensitive is my budget to a payment increase? (Very sensitive → fixed-rate home equity loan)
3. What is my plan if home values decline 15–20%?
4. Do I have the discipline to avoid drawing more than I need on a revolving line?
5. Have I compared rates from multiple lenders? (Rates vary significantly between institutions)
6. Is the purpose of the loan one that qualifies for potential interest deductibility? (Consult a tax advisor)

Both home equity loans and HELOCs can be appropriate, cost-effective financing when used with clear purpose and realistic repayment planning. The decision ultimately hinges on whether you need certainty (home equity loan) or flexibility (HELOC) — and how much variable-rate risk you are willing to absorb.
