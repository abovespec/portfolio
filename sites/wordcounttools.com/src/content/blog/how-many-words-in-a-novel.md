The "right" word count for a novel isn't arbitrary — it reflects what readers expect, what publishers can price and print profitably, and what the story actually needs. Here's the standard guidance by genre, with the reasoning behind the numbers.

## The general range

Most adult fiction novels run **70,000 to 110,000 words**. That's the range agents and publishers consider market-ready for debut authors. Significantly shorter (under 50,000) or longer (over 120,000) draws immediate scrutiny unless the genre has different norms.

## Word count by genre

| Genre | Standard range | Notes |
|-------|---------------|-------|
| Literary fiction | 80,000–100,000 | Can go to 120,000 if the writing justifies it |
| Commercial/mainstream fiction | 70,000–100,000 | Tighter range for debut authors |
| Mystery/crime thriller | 70,000–90,000 | Pacing matters; overly long thrillers drag |
| Psychological thriller | 80,000–100,000 | Slightly more room for atmosphere |
| Romance | 70,000–100,000 | Category romance (Harlequin) can be 50,000–70,000 |
| Science fiction | 90,000–120,000 | World-building requires space |
| Fantasy (epic) | 100,000–150,000+ | High tolerance for length in established authors |
| Fantasy (debut) | 100,000–120,000 | Push for 120,000+ only if story demands it |
| Horror | 70,000–100,000 | |
| Historical fiction | 80,000–110,000 | Research and setting add length |
| Women's fiction | 80,000–100,000 | |
| Upmarket/book club fiction | 85,000–100,000 | |
| Young Adult (YA) | 70,000–90,000 | Can reach 100,000 in fantasy/SF |
| Middle Grade | 25,000–50,000 | Shorter for younger readers |
| Chapter books | 4,000–10,000 | Ages 6–10 |
| Picture books | 500–1,000 | Text only; the rest is illustration |

## Why word count matters to publishers

**Printing costs:** Print-on-demand and traditional offset printing both have per-page costs. A 150,000-word debut fantasy novel costs significantly more to print than an 80,000-word thriller, making it harder to price competitively and sell enough copies to earn out the advance.

**Debut author risk:** Publishers take less risk on debut authors. An unknown author submitting a 200,000-word epic is asking for more shelf space, more printing cost, and more reader commitment — all without a proven track record. Once you have a readership, length norms relax.

**Reader expectations:** Genre readers have internalized expectations. A cozy mystery that runs 120,000 words will feel bloated to most readers in that genre, where 65,000–80,000 is the comfortable norm. Length signals pacing and tone.

## Notable exceptions

Word counts in published bestsellers vary widely:

- *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* — ~46,000 words (very short for SF)
- *Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone* — ~77,000 words
- *Harry Potter and the Order of the Phoenix* — ~257,000 words (J.K. Rowling had earned that by book 5)
- *War and Peace* — ~580,000 words
- *Atlas Shrugged* — ~565,000 words
- *The Fellowship of the Ring* — ~177,000 words

Established authors get more latitude. The rules for debut authors are tighter.

## NaNoWriMo and the 50,000-word mark

National Novel Writing Month (NaNoWriMo) targets 50,000 words in November. That's a valid first draft for some categories (middle grade, novellas, short literary fiction) but is under-length for most adult commercial fiction.

A 50,000-word NaNoWriMo draft is usually:
- A complete story with room to expand
- ~80% of the story that needs to be told (many NaNo finishers add 20,000–40,000 words in revision)
- A complete draft for shorter categories

Don't submit a 50,000-word manuscript as a finished novel unless your genre supports that length.

## How to estimate your current count

Every word processing app counts words differently (Microsoft Word, Google Docs, and Scrivener have slightly different tokenization rules for hyphenated words and special characters). For a consistent count:

- Use Scrivener's project count for works in progress
- Use Google Docs or Word for finished drafts
- For platform-agnostic counting, paste sections into [wordcounttools.com](/) for a character-exact count alongside reading time and readability

## What if your draft is over- or under-length?

**Too long (first draft):** This is common and usually fixable. Look for:
- Scenes that don't move the plot or reveal character
- Subplots that don't connect to the main theme
- Long paragraphs of setup that could be cut or condensed
- Dialogue that circles back instead of progressing

**Too short (first draft):** Less common but occurs in literary fiction and when authors rush to plot. Look for:
- Characters who could have stronger interiority
- Scenes that jump over conflict instead of dwelling in it
- Setting that's underdeveloped
- A second subplot that would deepen the theme

Word count is a rough constraint, not the goal. The goal is the story working at every level — word count is a signal that something may be off, not an end in itself.
