The average adult reads between 200 and 250 words per minute for general, everyday prose. That figure comes from decades of reading research — but the real picture is more nuanced, and knowing it helps you write, plan, and estimate reading time accurately.

## The benchmark numbers

| Reader type | WPM range |
|-------------|-----------|
| Average adult (general prose) | 200–250 |
| College student | 250–350 |
| Proficient adult reader | 300–400 |
| Speed reader | 400–700+ |
| Child (age 8–10) | 100–150 |
| ESL / second-language reader | 150–220 |
| Slow adult reader | 100–150 |

For more on this topic, see [*What Is Flesch-Kincaid Readability? A Plain-Language Guide*](/blog/what-is-flesch-kincaid-readability).

The 200–250 WPM figure is the one most reading-time calculators use because it represents the median for the broadest audience. Medium uses 265 WPM. Some tools use 200 WPM to be conservative.

A key 2016 study by Rayner et al. (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) found that college students average around 300 WPM for silent reading of standard prose — somewhat faster than the commonly cited 200 figure. The gap exists because many studies measure "functional reading" (reading for comprehension and retention), which is slower than rapid skimming.

## How content type changes reading speed

The 200–250 WPM benchmark assumes readable prose: standard sentences, familiar vocabulary, clean layout. Content difficulty significantly changes the speed:

| Content type | Typical reading speed |
|-------------|----------------------|
| Light fiction / easy news | 250–350 WPM |
| General nonfiction / blog posts | 200–250 WPM |
| Textbooks | 150–200 WPM |
| Technical documentation / code | 100–150 WPM |
| Academic papers | 100–180 WPM |
| Dense legal or scientific text | 50–120 WPM |

Code, formulas, tables, and diagrams break the linear reading flow. A programmer working through technical documentation will read far slower than 200 WPM — not because of skill, but because each line requires processing, not just scanning.

## How to measure your own reading speed

**Step 1:** Choose a passage of known length. A typical newspaper article or a section from a book works well. Use a [word count tool](/) to confirm the word count if you're unsure.

**Step 2:** Read at your normal pace — the pace you'd use to actually absorb and remember the content. Don't race.

**Step 3:** Time yourself in seconds, then calculate:

```
WPM = (Word count ÷ Time in seconds) × 60
```

**Example:** You read 450 words in 2 minutes and 10 seconds (130 seconds).

For more on this topic, see [*How Long Does It Take to Read 1,000 Words?*](/blog/how-long-to-read-1000-words).

```
WPM = (450 ÷ 130) × 60 = 207 WPM
```

**Step 4:** Test comprehension. Answer a few questions about what you read. Speed without comprehension doesn't count.

Repeat with different content types — your technical documentation speed will likely be much lower than your fiction speed.

## Reading speed vs. reading rate: what actually matters

Raw WPM isn't the right metric for most purposes. What matters is **comprehension-adjusted reading rate** — how much you actually retain per minute.

Research consistently finds that speed reading techniques (rapid serial visual presentation, removing subvocalization) increase WPM but decrease comprehension proportionally. There is no evidence that humans can reliably read at 1,000+ WPM with normal comprehension. The tradeoff is real.

For most practical purposes:

- **Skimming:** 600–700 WPM, low comprehension (~50%). Good for deciding whether to read something.
- **Regular reading:** 200–300 WPM, solid comprehension (~70–80%). Good for learning and retention.
- **Study reading:** 100–150 WPM, high comprehension (~85–90%). Good for technical or academic material you need to retain.

## Why WPM matters for content creators

Knowing your audience's reading speed (or a reasonable proxy for it) lets you estimate reading time — which has real implications for content planning.

For more on this topic, see [*Reading Time Calculator: How Long Will Readers Spend on Your Content?*](/blog/reading-time-calculator).

**Estimating reading time:**

```
Reading time (minutes) = Word count ÷ WPM
```

At 230 WPM (a reasonable default):

| Word count | Reading time |
|------------|-------------|
| 300 | ~1.3 min |
| 500 | ~2.2 min |
| 800 | ~3.5 min |
| 1,000 | ~4.5 min |
| 1,500 | ~6.5 min |
| 2,000 | ~8.5 min |
| 3,000 | ~13 min |
| 5,000 | ~22 min |

Displaying reading time ("5 min read") in article headers sets reader expectations upfront. Research from Medium and other publishers shows that readers who know the read time in advance have higher completion rates — they've opted into spending the time before they start.

## Reading speed by medium

Silent reading of printed text and digital screen reading have different benchmark speeds:

- **Print:** 250–300 WPM (research baseline)
- **Screen / desktop:** 200–250 WPM (~20–30% slower than print in some studies)
- **Mobile:** 150–200 WPM (smaller screen, more scrolling, more interruptions)
- **E-reader / tablet:** 220–270 WPM (similar to print; minimal glare)

The screen vs. print gap has narrowed significantly with modern display technology, but mobile reading remains slower due to environmental factors and attention fragmentation rather than pure display quality.

## What 200 WPM looks like in practice

At 200 WPM:
- A 500-word email takes 2.5 minutes to read
- A 10-page report (double-spaced, ~2,500 words) takes about 12.5 minutes
- A 300-page book (~90,000 words) takes about 7.5 hours of reading time

At 300 WPM (faster reader):
- The same 500-word email takes 1.7 minutes
- The 10-page report takes 8.3 minutes
- The 300-page book takes 5 hours

For newsletters, blog posts, and content marketing, these numbers are worth keeping in mind when writing. A 3,000-word deep-dive represents a 10–15 minute commitment for the average reader — which is appropriate for cornerstone content but too long for a routine update.
