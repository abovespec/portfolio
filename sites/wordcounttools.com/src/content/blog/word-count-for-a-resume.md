A resume has one job: get you an interview. Every word either earns its place or steals space from something that does. Knowing the right length isn't about hitting a number — it's about understanding what hiring managers actually read.

## How Many Words Should a Resume Be?

The practical word count ranges by experience level:

| Experience Level | Pages | Approximate Word Count |
|-----------------|-------|----------------------|
| Entry-level / Recent grad | 1 page | 300–500 words |
| Mid-career (3–10 years) | 1–2 pages | 475–900 words |
| Senior / Executive (10+ years) | 2 pages | 800–1,200 words |
| Academic / Federal CV | 3+ pages | 1,500+ words |

The word count ranges look wide because resumes aren't prose — they're structured data. A one-page resume might have 400 words in bullet points or 600 if you use fuller sentences. What matters more than the total count is the ratio of useful content to filler.

## Why One Page Is Still the Default

For anyone with fewer than 10 years of experience, a single page signals discipline. It shows you can prioritize. Recruiters at large companies often spend under 10 seconds on an initial scan; a tight one-pager means everything they see is intentional.

The one-page rule has exceptions:

- **Academic positions** require a CV listing publications, teaching, and grants — often 3–5+ pages.
- **Federal government jobs** use USA Jobs formats that explicitly require longer descriptions.
- **Executive roles** where the breadth of responsibilities genuinely can't compress to one page.

For everyone else, if your resume is running to two pages, try cutting before adding a second sheet.

## What to Cut to Hit Your Target Length

**Objective statements:** The "Seeking a challenging role where I can contribute..." section costs 30–50 words and adds nothing. Replace with a 2–3 sentence summary that leads with your strongest qualification, or remove it entirely.

**Duties vs. achievements:** "Responsible for managing social media accounts" is filler. "Grew Instagram followers from 4,200 to 28,000 in 14 months" is an achievement. Duty descriptions use more words and say less. Convert every bullet to an outcome where possible.

**Ancient jobs:** For experienced candidates, jobs from more than 15 years ago can be listed as one line (title, company, dates) with no bullets, or removed entirely. Your skills from 2005 are less relevant than your last role.

**Redundant tech stacks:** "Proficient in Microsoft Word, Excel, and PowerPoint" costs 10 words and signals nothing. Most professionals are assumed to know standard office software. List only tools that are specific to the role or demonstrate specialized capability.

**Soft skills paragraphs:** "Excellent communicator with strong teamwork skills" belongs in your cover letter if anywhere. Resumes prove it through examples, not assertions.

## What Dense Resumes Do Right

The highest-performing resumes aren't thin — they're dense with the right information. Each bullet follows a pattern:

> **[Action verb] + [what you did] + [measurable result]**

> *Reduced customer onboarding time by 40% by redesigning the intake form and automating email follow-ups.*

That's 18 words with a concrete result. It earns its place. Compare it to:

> *Helped improve customer onboarding processes and communications.*

Eight words, zero useful signal.

## Word Count by Resume Section

A typical mid-career resume breaks down roughly like this:

| Section | Word Count |
|---------|-----------|
| Header (name, contact, links) | 20–40 |
| Summary / profile | 40–80 |
| Work experience (2–4 roles) | 250–500 |
| Education | 30–60 |
| Skills | 30–60 |
| Optional (certifications, projects) | 40–100 |
| **Total** | **410–840** |

If your work experience section alone is over 600 words and you have fewer than 10 years of history, you're probably over-explaining each role. Three to five bullets per job is typically enough; more than six per role starts to read as a job description rather than an achievement summary.

## ATS vs. Human Reader Word Counts

Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) parse resumes before a human sees them. For ATS optimization, word count matters less than keyword density — specifically, whether your resume includes the exact phrases from the job description.

This creates a tension: keyword-stuffed resumes that score well in ATS often read poorly to humans. The balance is to use the natural language of the job description in your bullet points, not to add a separate "keywords" section with 50 terms.

A resume that reads well to a human and uses job-relevant language will typically score adequately in most ATS systems without needing to inflate word count.
