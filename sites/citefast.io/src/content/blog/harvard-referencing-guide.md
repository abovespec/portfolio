Harvard referencing is one of the most widely used citation systems in higher education, particularly in UK, Australian, and South African universities. Unlike APA or MLA, "Harvard" is not a single official standard maintained by one organization — it's a family of author-date styles with significant variation between institutions. What they share is the core format: the author's surname and year of publication appear in the text, and full details go in a reference list at the end.

This guide covers the most common Harvard conventions. Always check your institution's specific Harvard guide, since details like punctuation and formatting vary.

## How Harvard Referencing Works

Harvard is an **author-date** system. When you cite a source in your text, you include the author's surname and the publication year in parentheses. Your full reference list at the end gives readers everything they need to locate the original source.

In-text citation examples:
- One author: (Smith, 2021)
- Two authors: (Smith and Jones, 2021)
- Three or more authors: (Smith et al., 2021)
- Direct quote: (Smith, 2021, p. 45)

The reference list is arranged alphabetically by the author's surname and is usually headed "References" or "Bibliography."

## Citing Books

### Single Author

**In-text:** (Brown, 2019)

**Reference list:**
Brown, T. (2019) *Change by Design: How Design Thinking Transforms Organizations and Inspires Innovation*. 2nd edn. New York: HarperCollins.

### Two Authors

**In-text:** (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979)

**Reference list:**
Kahneman, D. and Tversky, A. (1979) 'Prospect theory: An analysis of decision under risk', *Econometrica*, 47(2), pp. 263–291.

### Three or More Authors

**In-text:** (Thompson et al., 2020)

**Reference list:**
Thompson, J., Davis, L., Wilson, M. and Carter, R. (2020) *Introduction to Research Methods*. London: SAGE Publications.

Note: some Harvard styles abbreviate three-or-more-author references in-text as "et al." but list all authors in the reference list. Check your institution's preference.

### Edited Book

**In-text:** (Black, 2018)

**Reference list:**
Black, P. (ed.) (2018) *Assessment for Learning*. Buckingham: Open University Press.

### Chapter in an Edited Book

**In-text:** (Freire, 2000)

**Reference list:**
Freire, P. (2000) 'The "banking" concept of education', in Bhattacharya, H. (ed.) *Literacy, Language and Power*. New York: Routledge, pp. 67–88.

## Citing Journal Articles

### Print Journal

**In-text:** (Seligman, 2011)

**Reference list:**
Seligman, M.E.P. (2011) 'Building resilience', *Harvard Business Review*, 89(4), pp. 100–106.

### Online Journal Article with DOI

**In-text:** (Dweck, 2008)

**Reference list:**
Dweck, C.S. (2008) 'Brainology: Transforming students' motivation to learn', *Independent School*, 67(2), pp. 1–5. doi: 10.1177/0956797617741719.

DOIs are preferred over URLs for journal articles because they are permanent and more reliable. Format the DOI as a full URL: `https://doi.org/10.xxxx/xxxxx` or simply `doi: 10.xxxx/xxxxx`.

## Citing Websites and Online Sources

### General Website

**In-text:** (NHS, 2023)

**Reference list:**
NHS (2023) *Type 2 diabetes*. Available at: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/type-2-diabetes/ (Accessed: 14 March 2023).

Key elements for websites:
- Author or organization
- Year of publication or last update
- Title of the page (italics)
- Available at: URL
- (Accessed: date)

### Web Page with No Author

When no author is identifiable, use the organization or website name.

**In-text:** (BBC, 2022)

**Reference list:**
BBC (2022) *How climate change affects the ocean*. Available at: https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/science-environment-56901261 (Accessed: 2 February 2022).

### Web Page with No Date

Use "no date" or "n.d." when no publication date is available.

**In-text:** (Oxford University, no date)

**Reference list:**
Oxford University (no date) *About the University*. Available at: https://www.ox.ac.uk/about (Accessed: 10 April 2023).

## Citing Other Source Types

### Government Report

**In-text:** (ONS, 2022)

**Reference list:**
ONS (Office for National Statistics) (2022) *UK Labour Market Statistics*. London: ONS. Available at: https://www.ons.gov.uk (Accessed: 15 October 2022).

### Newspaper Article

**In-text:** (Guardian, 2023) or (Jones, 2023)

**Reference list:**
Jones, A. (2023) 'Scientists warn of tipping points in climate system', *The Guardian*, 15 January, p. 4.

For online newspaper:
Jones, A. (2023) 'Scientists warn of tipping points in climate system', *The Guardian*, 15 January. Available at: https://www.theguardian.com/... (Accessed: 15 January 2023).

### Conference Paper

**In-text:** (Lee, 2021)

**Reference list:**
Lee, S. (2021) 'Machine learning in clinical diagnostics', in *Proceedings of the International Conference on Medical AI*, London, 12–14 June, pp. 112–119.

### Thesis or Dissertation

**In-text:** (Patel, 2020)

**Reference list:**
Patel, R. (2020) *The role of emotional intelligence in organizational leadership*. PhD thesis. University of Manchester.

## Reference List Format

### General Rules

- **Alphabetical order** by author surname (or title if no author)
- **Hanging indent**: the first line of each reference is flush left; subsequent lines are indented
- All authors are listed (don't truncate to "et al." in the reference list itself in most Harvard styles)
- Use italics for book titles and journal names
- Article titles are not italicized but may use single quotation marks

### Alphabetizing

- If multiple works by the same author, order by year (oldest first)
- If same author and same year, add letters: (Smith, 2021a), (Smith, 2021b)
- Ignore "A," "An," or "The" when alphabetizing by title

## Common Harvard Mistakes

**Using the wrong style variant**: Your university may use a specific version (Leeds Harvard, Anglia Ruskin Harvard, APA-Harvard, etc.). Always download your institution's specific guide.

**Missing access dates for websites**: Always include the date you accessed an online source — web pages can change or disappear.

**Inconsistent punctuation**: Harvard styles vary on details like whether to put a period after the year, whether to use "pp." for page numbers, and how to format edition numbers. Pick one and be consistent.

**Confusing author-date for author-page**: Harvard uses (Author, Year). MLA uses (Author page). Don't mix them.

**Incomplete website references**: Include all four elements — author/org, year, title (italicized), URL, and access date.

## Harvard vs. APA

Harvard and APA 7th edition look very similar because both are author-date systems. Key differences:

| Feature | Harvard (typical) | APA 7th |
|---------|-------------------|---------|
| Three-or-more authors | et al. from first citation | et al. from first citation |
| Organization names | Abbreviated in-text (optional) | Full name first use, abbreviation after |
| DOI format | doi: 10.xxx or full URL | https://doi.org/10.xxx |
| Edition | "2nd edn" | (2nd ed.) |
| Running head | Not required | Not required |

When in doubt about which style your institution requires, check your course handbook or ask your librarian. Many UK universities have their own Harvard guide — it's worth downloading the specific version.
