In-text citations are the core of APA style. Every time you quote, paraphrase, or summarize information from a source, you include a brief citation in the text that points your reader to the full reference in your reference list. APA 7th edition uses the **author-date** format: the author's last name and the year of publication appear in parentheses.

This guide covers every in-text citation scenario you're likely to encounter.

## The Basic Format

An APA in-text citation includes:
1. The author's last name
2. The year of publication
3. A page number (only required for direct quotes)

**Parenthetical citation** (at the end of a sentence):
> Emotional regulation significantly predicts academic performance (Zimmerman, 2000).

**Narrative citation** (the author's name is part of your sentence):
> Zimmerman (2000) found that emotional regulation significantly predicts academic performance.

Both forms are acceptable. Narrative citations work well when you want to emphasize the researcher. Parenthetical citations keep the focus on the information itself.

## Number of Authors

### One Author

**Parenthetical:** (Kahneman, 2011)

**Narrative:** Kahneman (2011) argues that...

### Two Authors

Always cite both names, joined by "and" in narrative form and "&" in parenthetical form.

**Parenthetical:** (Smith & Jones, 2019)

**Narrative:** Smith and Jones (2019) demonstrated that...

### Three or More Authors

Use only the first author's name followed by "et al." from the very first citation.

**Parenthetical:** (Williams et al., 2020)

**Narrative:** Williams et al. (2020) suggest that...

**Exception:** If two different references would shorten to the same "Author et al., Year" form, include enough authors to distinguish them:
- (Thompson, Davis, et al., 2020)
- (Thompson, Wilson, et al., 2020)

### Group or Organization as Author

First use: spell out the full name, with abbreviation in brackets.

> ...(American Psychological Association [APA], 2020)...

Subsequent uses:

> ...(APA, 2020)...

If a group author doesn't have a well-known abbreviation, always spell it out in full.

## Direct Quotes

When you reproduce an author's exact words, you must include a **page number** (or paragraph number for sources without page numbers).

### Short Quotes (fewer than 40 words)

Incorporate into your text with quotation marks:

> Dweck (2006) concluded that "the view you adopt for yourself profoundly affects the way you lead your life" (p. 6).

> Intelligence is better understood as "a dynamic quality rather than a fixed trait" (Blackwell et al., 2007, p. 248).

### Long Quotes (40 words or more)

Use a **block quote**: indent the entire quotation 0.5 inches from the left margin, no quotation marks, and place the citation after the final punctuation.

> Vygotsky's (1978) zone of proximal development remains central to contemporary theories of learning:
>
>     Every function in the child's cultural development appears twice: first, on the social level, and later, on the individual level; first, between people (interpsychological) and then inside the child (intrapsychological). (p. 57)

### Page Numbers for Different Source Types

- **Book/article page:** p. 45 or pp. 45–47 (for a range)
- **No page numbers (website, ebook):** Use paragraph number (para. 3), heading and paragraph (Introduction section, para. 2), or a heading abbreviation
- **eBook with location numbers (Kindle):** Use chapter number if available — Chapter 3

## Paraphrasing and Summarizing

When you put another author's idea in your own words, you cite the author and year but do **not** need a page number (though APA 7 encourages it when it helps the reader locate the passage).

> Students who attribute failure to effort rather than ability demonstrate greater persistence (Dweck, 2006).

A page number is optional here but welcomed:

> Students who attribute failure to effort rather than ability demonstrate greater persistence (Dweck, 2006, p. 12).

## Multiple Sources in One Citation

List citations alphabetically by first author, separated by semicolons:

> Several studies have examined this relationship (Brown, 2018; Davis & Lee, 2020; Thompson et al., 2019).

## Multiple Works by the Same Author

### Different Years

Simply cite both, separated by a comma:

> (Smith, 2018, 2021)

### Same Year

Add lowercase letters after the year, matching your reference list:

> (Smith, 2020a, 2020b)

In your reference list, the letters correspond to the alphabetical order of the titles.

## Secondary Sources (Citing a Source You Didn't Read)

When you want to cite a source that was quoted or summarized in another work (and you can't access the original), use "as cited in":

> Piaget's cognitive development theory (as cited in Woolfolk, 2019) suggests...

Only the source you actually read (Woolfolk, 2019) appears in your reference list. Use secondary sources sparingly — always try to locate the original.

## Personal Communications

Emails, interviews, phone calls, and non-retrievable messages are cited in-text only — they do not appear in the reference list.

> According to J. Smith (personal communication, March 15, 2023), the project is scheduled for...

## Sources with No Author

Use the first few words of the title in place of the author name. Italicize book/report titles; use quotation marks for article/chapter titles.

**Book or report:** (*Style Manual*, 2021)

**Article or chapter:** ("New Evidence," 2022)

## Sources with No Date

Use "n.d." (no date):

> (National Health Service, n.d.)

If the content is likely to change (like a website), add a retrieval date in the reference list entry.

## Republished or Translated Works

Cite both dates: original first, republished/translated second:

> (Freud, 1900/1999)

In the reference list, include both dates as well.

## Common In-Text Citation Mistakes

**Adding "p." before every year**: Only use "p." for direct quotes. Paraphrases don't require it (though it's encouraged).

**Using "ibid."**: APA does not use ibid. Simply repeat the citation each time.

**Missing the year**: APA always requires the year. "According to Smith, emotional regulation..." is incomplete — it must be "According to Smith (2000), ..."

**Punctuation placement**: The period goes *after* the closing parenthesis:
- Correct: ...predicts performance (Zimmerman, 2000).
- Wrong: ...predicts performance. (Zimmerman, 2000)

**Misusing et al.**: In APA 7, use "et al." for three or more authors from the first citation. APA 6 used et al. only after the first full citation — APA 7 simplified this.

## Quick Reference Table

| Situation | In-Text Format |
|-----------|----------------|
| 1 author | (Brown, 2020) |
| 2 authors | (Brown & Lee, 2020) |
| 3+ authors | (Brown et al., 2020) |
| Organization | (WHO, 2021) |
| No author | (*Title*, 2021) or ("Article Title," 2021) |
| No date | (Brown, n.d.) |
| Direct quote | (Brown, 2020, p. 45) |
| Secondary source | (Jones, as cited in Brown, 2020) |
| Same author, same year | (Brown, 2020a, 2020b) |
| Personal communication | (J. Brown, personal communication, May 1, 2020) |
